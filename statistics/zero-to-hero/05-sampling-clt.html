Sampling Distributions & the CLT

This is the hinge of the whole course. A statistic changes from sample to sample — so it has a distribution of its own. Knowing the shape and width of that distribution is what lets us attach an error bar to any estimate.

~30 min Data: 142 Norwegian fields (the population) · Brent daily returns

In 60 seconds

  • Sampling error = statistic − parameter. It is unavoidable and random.
  • The sampling distribution of x̄ is the distribution of all possible sample means for a given n. Its mean is μ; its SD — the standard error — is σ/√n.
  • Central limit theorem: for large n, x̄ is approximately normal whatever the shape of the population.
  • "Large" depends on the skew: n = 30 is plenty for mild skew and not enough for oil-field sizes.

Sampling error, made visible

Take a tiny population — six Norwegian fields — so we can list every possible sample. With samples of size 3 there are C(6,3) = 20 of them. Each gives a different x̄; the collection of all 20 is the sampling distribution.

Mean and standard error of x̄

Center

\mu_{\bar{x}} = \mu

x̄ is an unbiased estimator: on average it hits the target.

Standard error

\sigma_{\bar{x}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}

Quadruple the sample to halve the error. For sampling without replacement where n is over 5 % of N, multiply by \sqrt{\tfrac{N - n}{N - 1}}.

The central limit theorem, live

The population below is the real list of Norwegian field sizes: mean 79.0, SD 191.0, skewness 6.1 — about as far from a bell curve as real data gets. Draw thousands of samples and watch the histogram of their means.

Orange curve = the normal curve the CLT predicts: mean μ, SD σ/√n.

How big is "large enough"?

Server-side simulation (20,000 samples per size, with replacement) quantifies how fast the skew of x̄ disappears for field sizes:

nMean of x̄SD of x̄ (simulated)σ/√n (theory)Skewness of x̄

The standard error formula is spot-on at every n. Normality is not: the skewness only falls like 1/√n, so with a population skewness of 6 you need n ≈ 60 or more before x̄ looks symmetric. The common "n ≥ 30" rule assumes mild skew. For extreme skew, use larger samples, transform the data (logs), or use the bootstrap (chapter 12).

Do it in code

f = pd.read_sql("select recoverable_oe_msm3 as oe from ncs_field_reserves where recoverable_oe_msm3 > 0", con).oe.values
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
means = np.array([rng.choice(f, 30).mean() for _ in range(20_000)])
means.mean(), means.std()          # ≈ μ = 79.0 and ≈ σ/√30 = 34.9
stats.skew(means)                  # ≈ 1.1  -> still visibly skewed at n = 30
select round(avg(xbar), 2) as mean_of_means, round(stddev_samp(xbar), 2) as standard_error
from (select s, avg(f.recoverable_oe_msm3) as xbar
      from generate_series(1, 2000) as s
      cross join lateral (select recoverable_oe_msm3
                          from ncs_field_reserves
                          where recoverable_oe_msm3 > 0 and s > 0      -- reference s: new sample per row
                          order by random() limit 30) f
      group by s) t;      -- without replacement: SE ≈ 34.9 × √((142−30)/141) ≈ 31

Key takeaways

  • A statistic has a sampling distribution; its SD is the standard error.
  • x̄ is unbiased and its SE is σ/√n — precision grows with the square root of n.
  • CLT: x̄ becomes normal for large n regardless of the population — but skewed populations need much larger n.
  • Simulation is the honest way to check "is n large enough?" for your data.

Self-check

1 · Standard error

Brent monthly returns have SD ≈ 10 %. What is the standard error of the mean monthly return over 10 years (120 months)?

Answer

10 / √120 = 0.91 percentage points. A 10-year average monthly return of +0.4 % is therefore well within noise of zero.

2 · Halving the error

An estimate from n = 50 wells has SE 3.5 days. How many wells for SE ≈ 1.75 days?

Answer

Four times as many: n = 200, because SE ∝ 1/√n.

3 · True or false

"The CLT says that with a large sample, the data become normally distributed."

Answer

False. The data keep the population's shape; it is the distribution of the sample mean that becomes normal.
