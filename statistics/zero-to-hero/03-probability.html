Chapter 03 · Probability

The Probability Toolkit

Probability is the language of uncertainty — and exploration is the most uncertain business in energy. This chapter builds the whole toolkit (rules, tables, conditioning, Bayes, counting) on one real question: what are the odds that a Norwegian wildcat finds hydrocarbons?

~30 min Data: 1,380 Norwegian wildcat wells with a reported result

In 60 seconds

  • A probability is a long-run relative frequency, between 0 (impossible) and 1 (certain).
  • Addition rule for "or", multiplication rule for "and", complement for "not": P(not E) = 1 − P(E).
  • Conditional probability P(B | A) restricts the world to cases where A happened. Events are independent when conditioning changes nothing.
  • Bayes' rule reverses a condition: from P(evidence | cause) to P(cause | evidence).

Experiments, outcomes and events

An experiment is any action with an uncertain result — drilling a wildcat. Its possible results form the sample space; an event is any set of outcomes ("finds oil or gas"). When every outcome is equally likely, the probability of an event is simply

Equal-likelihood (f / N) rule

P(E) = \frac{f}{N} = \frac{\text{outcomes in } E}{\text{possible outcomes}}

Pick one of the 1,380 wildcats at random: P(discovery) = 655 / 1,380 = 0.4746.

The frequentist interpretation reads that number as the proportion of discoveries you would see over a very long run of similar wells. Watch it settle:

Try it: drill virtual wildcats

Each well is a discovery with probability p = 0.4746 (the Norwegian record). The running share of discoveries wanders early, then locks onto p — the law of large numbers.

Combining events: Venn diagrams and the basic rules

Let N = "the well is in the North Sea" and D = "the well found hydrocarbons".

Complement rule

P(\text{not } E) = 1 - P(E)

P(dry or shows only) = 1 − 0.4746 = 0.5254. Handy for "at least one".

Special addition rule

P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B)

Only if A and B are mutually exclusive (e.g. two different sea areas).

General addition rule

P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \mathbin{\&} B)

P(N or D) = 0.6594 + 0.4746 − 0.3123 = 0.8217 — subtract the overlap you counted twice.

Conditional probability

Conditional probability rule

P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mathbin{\&} B)}{P(A)}

"Probability of B given A": shrink the world to the A row, then ask how often B happens there.

The three conditional rates are within two percentage points of the overall rate. For this question area and success look (nearly) independent — a surprise to many, given how different the provinces are. (What each area finds — oil versus gas — is a different story; chapter 09 tests it formally.)

Multiplication rule and independence

General multiplication rule

P(A \mathbin{\&} B) = P(A) \cdot P(B \mid A)

Always true. Follows directly from the conditional rule.

Independence

\begin{gathered} P(B \mid A) = P(B) \\ \iff P(A \mathbin{\&} B) = P(A)\,P(B) \end{gathered}

Special multiplication rule. Two independent wildcats both discover with probability 0.4746² = 0.2253.

Mutually exclusive ≠ independent

"North Sea" and "Barents Sea" are mutually exclusive — knowing one happened tells you the other did not, which is the opposite of independence. Two events with non-zero probability can never be both mutually exclusive and independent.

Independence across wells is an assumption, not a fact. Wells drilled on the same structure or by the same play concept share geology: a dry well raises the chance that its neighbour is dry. Treat "independent trials" as a model you must defend.

Total probability and Bayes' rule

Suppose a press release says only: "a new gas discovery offshore Norway". Which sea is it in? Before the news, the areas have prior probabilities (their share of wildcats). The news is evidence; Bayes' rule turns the per-area gas rates into posterior probabilities.

Rule of total probability

P(B) = \sum_j P(A_j)\, P(B \mid A_j)

A_1, \dots, A_k mutually exclusive and exhaustive (they cover every case).

Bayes' rule

P(A_i \mid B) = \frac{P(A_i)\, P(B \mid A_i)}{\sum_j P(A_j)\, P(B \mid A_j)}

Posterior ∝ prior × likelihood, normalised by the total probability of the evidence.

The Norwegian Sea holds 23 % of wildcats but 36 % of gas finds — the evidence shifts belief towards it.

Counting rules

When outcomes are equally likely, probability becomes counting. Three tools:

Basic counting rule

m_1 \cdot m_2 \cdots m_r

3 rig types × 4 well designs × 2 mud systems = 24 well plans.

Permutations (order matters)

{}_mP_r = \frac{m!}{(m - r)!}

Ways to schedule 3 of 8 prospects in order: 336.

Combinations (order ignored)

{}_mC_r = \binom{m}{r} = \frac{m!}{r!\,(m - r)!}

Ways to pick 5 of 12 licence blocks: 792.

Try it: a licence-round lottery

A round offers m blocks; k of them truly contain hydrocarbons. You pick r blocks blind. What is the chance that all your picks are winners, or that at least one is?

Do it in code

-- v_wildcats: one row per Norwegian wildcat with a reported result, discovery = true/false
select coalesce(main_area, 'ALL')                       as area,
       count(*) filter (where discovery)                as discovery,
       count(*) filter (where not discovery)            as no_discovery,
       round(count(*)::numeric / (select count(*) from v_wildcats), 4) as p_area,            -- marginal
       round(avg(discovery::int), 4)                    as p_disc_given_area                 -- conditional
from v_wildcats
group by rollup (main_area)
order by 1;
w = pd.read_sql("select main_area, discovery, fluid from v_wildcats", con)
joint = pd.crosstab(w.main_area, w.discovery, normalize="all", margins=True)   # joint + marginals
cond  = pd.crosstab(w.main_area, w.discovery, normalize="index")                # P(outcome | area)

# Bayes: P(area | gas found)
prior = w.main_area.value_counts(normalize=True)
lik   = w.groupby("main_area").fluid.apply(lambda s: (s == "gas").mean())
post  = prior * lik / (prior * lik).sum()

Key takeaways

  • Complement, addition (subtract the overlap) and multiplication (use the conditional) cover almost every calculation.
  • A contingency table gives joint, marginal and conditional probabilities in one place.
  • Independence means P(B | A) = P(B). It is a modelling choice to justify, not a default.
  • Bayes: posterior ∝ prior × likelihood. Thinking in counts makes it intuitive.

Self-check

1 · At least one

A company drills 4 independent wildcats, each with P(discovery) = 0.4746. What is P(at least one discovery)?

Answer

1 − P(all four dry) = 1 − 0.5254⁴ = 1 − 0.0762 = 0.924. The complement turns "at least one" into a single product.

2 · Read the table

Using the joint table, what is P(Barents Sea & discovery), and what is P(discovery | Barents Sea)?

Answer

Joint: 77 / 1,380 = 0.0558. Conditional: 77 / 157 = 0.4904. The joint probability is small because the Barents Sea itself is rare; the conditional rate is about the same as elsewhere.

3 · Exclusive or independent?

Are "the well found oil" and "the well found only gas" mutually exclusive, independent, or neither?

Answer

Mutually exclusive (one well cannot have both as its single reported category), and therefore not independent: knowing it found only gas makes "found oil" impossible.
