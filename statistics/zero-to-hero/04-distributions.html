Chapter 04 · Probability

Random Variables & Distributions

A distribution is a complete description of how an uncertain quantity behaves. Three of them — binomial, Poisson and normal — carry most of applied statistics. This chapter fits each to real energy data, and shows where real data refuses to cooperate.

~40 min Data: NCS wildcats · discoveries per year · EIA Brent daily · EIA refinery utilisation

In 60 seconds

  • A random variable assigns a number to each outcome; its distribution says which values occur with what probability.
  • Binomial: count of successes in n independent yes/no trials. Poisson: count of events in a fixed window. Normal: the bell curve for continuous measurements and averages.
  • The mean (expected value) and standard deviation summarise any distribution.
  • Always check the model against the data: normal probability plots and dispersion checks catch fat tails and clustering that break textbook formulas.

Discrete random variables

Let X = number of discoveries in a 10-wildcat drilling campaign. X can be 0, 1, …, 10. Its probability distribution lists P(X = x) for each value, and its probability histogram draws them as bars whose heights sum to 1.

Mean (expected value)

\mu = E(X) = \sum x \, P(X = x)

The long-run average of X over many campaigns.

Variance & SD

\sigma^2 = \sum (x - \mu)^2 \, P(X = x), \qquad \sigma = \sqrt{\sigma^2}

Equivalent shortcut: \sigma^2 = \sum x^2 P(x) - \mu^2.

The law of large numbers links the two worlds: the average of many observed values of X approaches μ. That is why expected value is the right yardstick for a portfolio of many prospects — and the wrong one for a single make-or-break well.

The binomial distribution

Each wildcat is a Bernoulli trial: two outcomes (discovery or not), the same success probability p each time, and trials independent of each other. The number of successes in n such trials is binomial.

Binomial probability

\begin{gathered} P(X = x) = \binom{n}{x} p^x (1 - p)^{n - x} \\[4pt] \binom{n}{x} = \frac{n!}{x!\,(n - x)!} \end{gathered}

The fraction is the binomial coefficient: how many orders give x successes.

Binomial mean & SD

\mu = np, \qquad \sigma = \sqrt{np(1 - p)}

With p = 0.5085 (2015–2024 Norwegian rate): μ = 5.08, σ = 1.58 for n = 10.

Sampling without replacement: the hypergeometric

The binomial assumes p stays fixed. When you draw from a small finite population without replacement (the licence-round lottery in chapter 03), each draw changes the odds; that count follows the hypergeometric distribution. If the sample is under about 5 % of the population, the binomial is an excellent approximation.

The Poisson distribution

Counts of events in a fixed window — discoveries per year, pipeline leaks per 1,000 km, unplanned refinery outages per quarter — are often modelled as Poisson: events occur independently at a constant average rate λ.

Poisson probability

P(X = x) = e^{-\lambda} \frac{\lambda^x}{x!}, \qquad \mu = \lambda, \quad \sigma = \sqrt{\lambda}

Also approximates the binomial when n is large and p small (λ = n p).

The data is overdispersed: the variance is more than twice the mean. Exploration activity rises and falls with the oil price and licensing rounds, so the rate λ is not constant. The Poisson model would call 2008–2009 a once-in-130-years event; in reality it was a boom. The fix — letting λ vary (a negative binomial model) — is a hero-level idea; spotting the misfit is the core skill.

Continuous variables and density curves

For continuous variables we describe probability by a density curve: a curve on or above the axis with total area 1. The probability that the variable falls between a and b is the area under the curve between a and b. (So any single exact value has probability 0.)

The normal distribution

A variable is normally distributed when its density is the symmetric bell curve determined by two parameters: the mean μ (center) and the standard deviation σ (spread; the inflection points sit at μ ± σ). Standardising, z = (x − μ)/σ, turns every normal into the standard normal (μ = 0, σ = 1), whose areas are tabulated — or, here, computed live.

Normal area calculator

Pre-set to U.S. weekly refinery utilisation in 2023: mean 90.05 %, SD 3.29 %. Shaded area = probability.

Empirical rule (normal only)

\begin{aligned} \mu \pm \sigma &: 68.26\% \\ \mu \pm 2\sigma &: 95.44\% \\ \mu \pm 3\sigma &: 99.74\% \end{aligned}

Inverse problem: percentiles

x = \mu + z_\alpha \, \sigma

z_\alpha leaves area α to its right: z_{0.05} = 1.645,\ z_{0.025} = 1.96,\ z_{0.005} = 2.576. The 95th percentile of utilisation ≈ 90.05 + 1.645 × 3.29 = 95.5 %.

Is it really normal? Normal probability plots

A normal probability plot (Q-Q plot) sorts the data and plots each value's z-score against where it should fall under a normal curve (its normal score). Normal data hugs the diagonal; curvature means skew; an S-shape with ends flaring away means fat tails; isolated points far off the line are outliers.

Moves beyond 4 SDs: a normal model expects 0.5 such days in the sample; Brent produced 50. A risk model built on the normal curve would be wrong by a factor of 100 exactly when it matters most. The Shapiro–Wilk normality test rejects normality for both daily and monthly returns (p < 0.0001).

Normal approximation to the binomial

For large n, the binomial histogram looks normal with μ = np and σ = √(np(1−p)). The rule of thumb: np and n(1−p) both at least 5. Because the binomial is discrete, use a continuity correction of 0.5: P(X ≥ 15) ≈ P(Y ≥ 14.5) for normal Y.

Key takeaways

  • Expected value and SD summarise any distribution; the law of large numbers makes the mean meaningful over many repeats.
  • Binomial = successes in n independent trials; Poisson = counts at a constant rate. Both rely on independence and constant rates — check them.
  • Probabilities for continuous variables are areas under a density curve; the normal curve is fixed by μ and σ.
  • Q-Q plots reveal fat tails; daily oil returns are strongly non-normal.

Self-check

1 · Expected discoveries

An operator plans 6 wildcats with p = 0.45 each. Expected number of discoveries and SD?

Answer

μ = 6 × 0.45 = 2.7; σ = √(6 × 0.45 × 0.55) = √1.485 = 1.22.

2 · Normal percentile

If utilisation ~ normal(90.05, 3.29), below what level do the worst 2.5 % of weeks fall?

Answer

x = 90.05 − 1.96 × 3.29 = 83.6 %. (Real weeks include hurricane shutdowns far below this — another tail the normal model underestimates.)

3 · Diagnose the plot

A Q-Q plot of field sizes curves upward sharply at the right end. What does that say?

Answer

Right skew: the largest values are far bigger than a normal curve allows. A log transformation usually straightens such data.
