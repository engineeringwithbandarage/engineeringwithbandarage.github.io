Statistics in One Picture

Statistics is the discipline of drawing reliable conclusions from incomplete, noisy data. Before any formula, you need a map of the territory: what we measure, who we measure, and the two very different jobs statistics does.

~20 min Data: Our World in Data energy panel (2023) · Norwegian Offshore Directorate wells

In 60 seconds

  • The population is everything you care about; a sample is the part you actually observe.
  • A number describing the population is a parameter (fixed, usually unknown). The same number computed on a sample is a statistic (known, but varies from sample to sample).
  • Descriptive statistics summarise the data you have. Inferential statistics use a sample to say something about the population — with a stated uncertainty.
  • How data was collected (random sample? experiment? observation?) decides what conclusions are allowed, more than any formula does.

In real work the population is almost never fully observed: all future wildcat wells, every barrel a refinery will run, all customers who will ever buy diesel. We see a sample, compute a statistic, and need to know how far that statistic can wander from the parameter. That single question drives chapters 05 to 12.

Notation you will see everywhere

Greek letters for parameters, Latin letters for statistics: μ (mean) ↔ x̄, σ (standard deviation) ↔ s, p (proportion) ↔ p̂, ρ (correlation) ↔ r, β (regression slope) ↔ b. N is the population size, n the sample size.

Observational study or designed experiment?

Observational study

We observe what happened without assigning anything. Example: comparing discovery rates between the North Sea and the Barents Sea. Nobody chose which geology each well got.

Allowed conclusion: association. Areas differ in water depth, era, operators and geology all at once — any of these lurking variables could explain a difference.

Designed experiment

We assign treatments at random. Example: a refinery tests two catalysts by randomly assigning 40 production runs to catalyst A or B and measuring gasoline yield.

Allowed conclusion: cause and effect — randomisation balances the lurking variables between the groups on average.

The language of experiments

A control group receives the baseline (e.g. the current catalyst) and the treatment group the new option. In a randomized block design the units are first split into homogeneous blocks (here: runs on similar crude), and treatments are randomized inside each block — the comparison is then not drowned by block-to-block noise.

How to draw a sample: four probability designs

A sample is representative when it mirrors the population on the features that matter. The only reliable way to get there is probability sampling — each unit has a known chance of selection. Suppose a regulator wants to audit 100 of the 1,380 Norwegian wildcat wells:

DesignHowWhen it shines
Simple random (SRS)Every set of 100 wells is equally likely. Without replacement is the norm; with replacement (SRSWR) lets a unit appear twice.Default; the basis of all formulas in this track.
SystematicOrder the list, pick a random start among the first k = N/n, then every k-th well.Easy in the field; risky if the list has a period matching k.
ClusterSplit into natural groups (e.g. production licences), randomly choose whole groups.When travel/access is the cost driver.
StratifiedSplit into strata (sea areas), take an SRS inside each — with proportional allocation, each stratum's share of the sample matches its share of the population.Guarantees every area is represented; usually more precise than SRS.

With proportional allocation the audit takes 66 North Sea, 23 Norwegian Sea and 11 Barents Sea wells. Real surveys often combine designs in stages (multistage sampling), e.g. pick licences, then wells inside them.

Survivorship bias in energy data

The Norwegian field-reserves list contains only developed or approved fields. Small discoveries that were never developed are missing. A study of "typical field size" based on that list is biased upward — the sample was filtered by the very outcome we measure. Always ask: what process decided which rows exist?

Do it in code

-- population parameter: mean energy use per person, all countries, 2023
select count(*) as n, round(avg(energy_per_capita)) as mu
from owid_country_energy
where year = 2023 and energy_per_capita is not null;

-- a simple random sample of 20 countries and its statistic x-bar
select round(avg(energy_per_capita)) as xbar
from (select energy_per_capita
      from owid_country_energy
      where year = 2023 and energy_per_capita is not null
      order by random()
      limit 20) s;

-- stratified sample: 100 wildcats, proportional to sea area
with w as (select *, row_number() over (partition by main_area order by random()) as rn,
                  count(*) over (partition by main_area) as n_area,
                  count(*) over () as n_all
           from v_wildcats)
select main_area, count(*) as picked
from w
where rn <= round(100.0 * n_area / n_all)
group by main_area;

Key takeaways

  • Parameter = population truth (fixed, unknown). Statistic = sample estimate (known, variable).
  • Descriptive statistics summarise; inferential statistics generalise with a measured uncertainty.
  • Only randomized experiments support causal claims; observational data shows association.
  • Probability sampling (SRS, systematic, cluster, stratified) is what makes a sample trustworthy. Always ask what process created your rows.

Self-check

1 · Parameter or statistic?

"The mean drilling time of the 295 Norwegian wildcats spudded 2015–2024 was 39.3 days." Treated as a sample of all future wildcats, is 39.3 a parameter or a statistic?

Answer

A statistic (x̄). The parameter would be the mean drilling time of the whole (future) population of wildcats, which is unknown.

2 · Can we claim cause?

Countries with a high share of renewables tend to have lower energy use per person. Does adding renewables reduce energy use?

Answer

Not from this data. It is observational: income, climate, industry mix and electrification all differ between countries and could drive both variables. Only an experiment or a careful causal design could support the causal claim.

3 · Pick the design

You must inspect 60 of 3,000 offshore pipeline segments, which are grouped into 40 pipelines. Visiting a pipeline is expensive; segments within a pipeline are cheap. Which design fits?

Answer

Cluster sampling (choose some pipelines at random, inspect segments within them), possibly in two stages. If pipelines differ systematically (age, material), stratifying the pipelines first improves precision.
