Chapter 02 · Foundations

Seeing Data

Before testing anything, look. This chapter turns raw columns into pictures and a handful of numbers — and shows, with real oil fields and oil prices, why the "average" is often the wrong number to report.

~35 min Data: Norwegian field reserves (142 fields) · EIA Brent daily prices · OWID 2023 · NCS wildcats

In 60 seconds

  • First classify each variable: qualitative (categories) or quantitative (numbers, discrete or continuous). The type decides the chart and the summary.
  • Describe a distribution by shape (symmetric / skewed, modality), center (mean, median) and spread (SD, IQR).
  • For skewed data — oil-field sizes, incomes, energy use — the median and IQR describe the typical case; the mean is dragged by a few giants.
  • A z-score puts any value on a common scale: how many standard deviations from the mean.

"Dry" is the mode (most frequent category), but add up the five hydrocarbon categories and discoveries are almost as common as failures. A pie chart would hide that comparison; bars sorted by size make it easy. Use a pie only for a part-to-whole view with at most about six slices.

Quantitative data: grouping into a histogram

For a continuous variable, split the range into classes (bins) of equal width, count the values falling in each, and draw adjacent bars. Each class has cutpoints (lower and upper boundary), a width and a midpoint. Too few classes hide the shape; too many make noise — 10 to 30 is a good starting range.

The shapes you will meet

The sample distribution you plot is an estimate of the population distribution of the variable. With more data, the histogram's outline converges to the population's shape — which is exactly what chapter 04 turns into density curves.

Center: mean, median, mode

Sample mean

\bar{x} = \frac{\sum x_i}{n}

Balance point. Uses every value — so one giant moves it.

Median

\tilde{x} = x_{\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)} \text{ of the sorted data}

Position (n+1)/2; average the two middle values when n is even. Resistant to outliers.

Trimmed mean

\bar{x}_{k\%} = \text{mean of the middle } (100 - 2k)\%

A compromise: resistant, yet uses most of the data.

The mean is four times the median. If a manager asks for "the typical Norwegian field", the median (~19) is the honest answer; the mean (~79) describes no real field. Rule of thumb: symmetric → mean; skewed or with outliers → median. For a right-skewed distribution, mean > median; for left-skewed, mean < median.

Spread: range, standard deviation, IQR

Sample variance & SD

s^2 = \frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2}{n - 1}, \qquad s = \sqrt{s^2}

Typical distance from the mean. n − 1 ("degrees of freedom") makes s² an unbiased estimate of σ².

Range & IQR

\text{Range} = \max - \min, \qquad \text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1

The range uses two extreme values only; the IQR covers the middle 50 % and is resistant.

Population versions

\mu = \frac{\sum x_i}{N}, \qquad \sigma = \sqrt{\frac{\sum (x_i - \mu)^2}{N}}

Divide by N when you hold the entire population.

How much data lies within k standard deviations?

Two rules connect the SD to the data. Chebyshev's rule holds for any distribution: at least 1 − 1/k² of the data lies within k SDs of the mean. The empirical rule (68–95–99.7) holds only for bell-shaped data. Brent's daily returns put both to the test:

±1 SDno guarantee68 %81.1%
±2 SD≥ 75 %95 %95.8%

Too many days are calm (81 % within one SD instead of 68 %) and too many are extreme (1.3 % beyond three SDs instead of 0.3 %). That "tall center, fat tails" signature is called leptokurtosis; the excess kurtosis here is 49 (0 for a normal curve). Remember it — it breaks naive risk models in chapter 04.

Quartiles, the five-number summary and boxplots

The p-th percentile has p % of the data below it; quartiles are the 25th, 50th and 75th percentiles (deciles and quintiles split into tenths and fifths). The five-number summary is min, Q1, median, Q3, max. Whiskers stretch to the most extreme values still inside the fences; anything outside is flagged as a potential outlier.

Boxplots shine at comparing groups. In a right-skewed distribution the median sits left of the box centre and the right whisker is longer; in a symmetric one the box and whiskers mirror each other.

Standardising: z-scores

z-score (standardised value)

z = \frac{x - \bar{x}}{s} \qquad \left(\text{population: } z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\right)

Distance from the mean in SD units. A standardised variable has mean 0 and SD 1.

Parameter or statistic? Population or sample?

The 216-country table is the full population of countries, so its mean and SD are the parameters μ and σ. The 142 Norwegian fields are the full population of developed Norwegian fields but only a sample of "oil fields in general". Same numbers, different roles — the role is decided by the question, not the data file.

Misleading graphs

A truncated axis is legitimate when the point is the size of changes (and the axis is clearly labelled), but it exaggerates them. Other classics: improper scaling (pictures sized by area or volume), dual axes that invent correlations, and cherry-picked time windows.

Key takeaways

  • Variable type → chart: bars for categories, histograms/boxplots for numbers.
  • Report shape, center and spread together. Skewed data → median and IQR.
  • Chebyshev always holds; the 68–95–99.7 rule only for bell shapes — and real price data has fat tails.
  • z-scores compare values across scales and flag unusual rows.
  • Check every chart's axis before believing its drama.

Self-check

1 · Which summary?

You report the "typical" country's oil production (50 producers; mean 1,038 TWh, median 357 TWh). Which number and why?

Answer

The median. Production is extremely right-skewed (the US, Saudi Arabia and Russia dominate), so the mean is pulled far above most producers. Pair it with the IQR (82–1,001 TWh).

2 · Read a z-score

A day's Brent return has z = −4. How rare would that be if returns were normal, and how often did it actually happen?

Answer

Under a normal curve, |z| > 4 happens with probability about 0.00006, i.e. about 0.5 days in our 8,400-day sample. It actually happened 50 times — about a hundred times more often.

3 · Fences

Using Q1 = 7.21 and Q3 = 71.18 for field sizes, which fields are potential outliers?

Answer

IQR = 63.97, upper fence = 71.18 + 1.5 × 63.97 = 167.1. Every field above about 167 million Sm³ o.e. is flagged (the lower fence is negative, so none on the low side). In skewed data "outlier" flags are expected and not errors — they are the giants that matter most.
