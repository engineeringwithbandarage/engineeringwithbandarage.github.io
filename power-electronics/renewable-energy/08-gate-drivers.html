Gate Drivers & Isolation

A microcontroller decides when each switch should turn on. Making it actually happen, in tens of nanoseconds, with the right voltage, at a high-side switch whose source jumps hundreds of volts every cycle, and safely shutting it down when something goes wrong, is the gate driver's job. Most destroyed power devices are not killed by the power circuit but by a weak or badly laid-out gate drive.

The gate is a capacitor

A MOSFET, IGBT or SiC MOSFET is controlled by the voltage on an insulated gate. Holding it on takes almost no current, but getting there means charging the gate capacitances with the total gate charge Q_g from the datasheet (Chapter 2). The speed of the switching transition is set by how fast the driver can push the Miller charge Q_{gd}, during which the drain voltage swings and the switching loss of Chapter 3 is incurred.

Peak gate current

I_{g,pk} = \frac{V_{on} - V_{off}}{R_{g,ext} + R_{g,int} + R_{drv}}

R_{g,int} is inside the device (datasheet); R_{drv} is the driver's output resistance.

Voltage transition time

t_{sw} \approx \frac{Q_{gd}}{I_{g,plateau}}

During the Miller plateau the gate voltage is stuck near V_{plateau}, so the current is (V_{on} - V_{plateau})/R_g.

Gate-drive power

P_{drv} = Q_g \, (V_{on} - V_{off}) \, f_s

Dissipated in the resistors and the driver, not in the switch.

Worked example: driving the boost's SiC MOSFET

A 650 V SiC MOSFET with Q_g = 60 nC and R_{g,int} = 4 Ω is driven between +18 V and −4 V at 50 kHz through R_{g,ext} = 5 Ω.

  1. Peak current: 22/(5 + 4) = 2.4 A, so a driver rated for at least 4 A peak leaves margin.
  2. Drive power: 60\,\text{nC} \times 22 \times 50\,\text{kHz} = 66 mW per switch: small, but the isolated supply feeding it must provide it.
  3. Resistor rating: most of that power heats the 5 Ω resistor, and its peak current is 2.4 A, so use a pulse-rated resistor, typically 0.25 W in a 1206 or larger package.

Choosing the gate resistor

The gate resistor is the main knob for switching speed. Smaller is faster, with lower switching loss, but brings higher dv/dt and di/dt, larger voltage overshoot from stray inductance, more ringing and more EMI (Chapter 10). Typical practice:

  • Separate turn-on and turn-off resistors, with a diode or a driver that has split outputs. Turn-on is often slowed to limit the diode's reverse-recovery current and the EMI it creates; turn-off is kept fast to cut loss and to hold the gate firmly off.
  • Start from the datasheet's test resistance, then adjust on the real board while measuring overshoot and ringing with a high-bandwidth probe.

Miller-induced turn-on

When one switch in a leg turns on, the switch node swings rapidly, and the other, off switch sees a large dv/dt across its drain. That drives a current through its gate-drain capacitance into its gate, and the voltage it develops across the turn-off path can lift the gate above threshold: a brief shoot-through that adds loss or destroys both devices.

Induced gate voltage

V_{GS,induced} \approx C_{gd} \, \frac{dv}{dt} \, (R_{g,off} + R_{g,int})

A first estimate that ignores C_{gs}, which in reality absorbs part of the current.

With C_{gd} = 10 pF and 50 V/ns, the injected current is 0.5 A; through 2 Ω + 4 Ω it develops 3 V, above a SiC MOSFET's threshold at high temperature (around 2 V). Three remedies, often combined:

  1. Negative turn-off voltage (−4 V here) moves the starting point well below threshold.
  2. An active Miller clamp: once the gate has fallen below about 2 V, the driver shorts it to the source through a low-impedance transistor, bypassing the gate resistor.
  3. Lower dv/dt through a slower turn-on of the opposite switch, at the cost of loss.

Driving the high-side switch

The low-side switch's source sits on the DC-link's negative rail, so its driver can share the controller's ground. The high-side switch's source is the switch node, which jumps between 0 and 400 V every cycle. Its driver must float with it. There are two ways to power and signal a floating driver.

Bootstrap supply

Bootstrap capacitor

C_{boot} \ge \frac{Q_g + I_{qbs} \, t_{on,max}}{\Delta V_{boot}}

I_{qbs}: the high-side driver's quiescent current; \Delta V_{boot}: the droop you accept before undervoltage lockout.

For our 60 nC switch, a driver drawing 100 µA, a maximum on-time of 20 µs and a 0.5 V droop: C_{boot} \ge (60 + 2)\,\text{nC} / 0.5\,\text{V} = 124 nF. Designers choose several times that, typically a 1 µF ceramic. The bootstrap diode must block the full DC-link voltage and recover quickly. The bootstrap is cheap and compact, but it has limits: the low-side switch must turn on regularly to recharge it (a problem at 100 % duty or at start-up), and it cannot easily provide the negative turn-off voltage SiC devices prefer.

Isolated drivers

The alternative is a driver whose input and output are galvanically isolated, using an optocoupler, an on-chip capacitive barrier or a coreless transformer, powered by an isolated supply, such as the flyback of Chapter 7 or a small isolated DC-DC module. This is standard for SiC, for IGBTs above a few kilowatts, and wherever regulations require reinforced isolation between the power circuit and the control.

  • Common-mode transient immunity (CMTI) is the dv/dt between the two sides that the driver tolerates without corrupting the signal. It must exceed the switch node's dv/dt with margin: at least 100 V/ns for SiC.
  • The isolated supply's coupling capacitance carries a common-mode current C \, dv/dt into the control ground every edge: 10 pF at 50 V/ns is 0.5 A. Choose supplies with a few picofarads of barrier capacitance.

Protection in the driver

  • Undervoltage lockout (UVLO). A switch driven with too little gate voltage runs partly on, in its linear region, and overheats in microseconds. The driver holds it off until its supply is healthy.
  • Desaturation (DESAT) detection. During a short circuit, an IGBT's collector voltage rises out of saturation while it is supposed to be on. The driver watches V_{CE} through a high-voltage diode after a blanking time of 1–3 µs and, if it exceeds about 7–9 V, turns the device off. IGBTs typically survive a short circuit for about 10 µs.
  • Soft turn-off. Interrupting a short-circuit current quickly through stray inductance would create a destructive voltage spike, so the driver turns off a faulted device slowly, through a larger resistance.
  • SiC and GaN need faster protection. SiC MOSFETs withstand short circuits for only about 2–3 µs, so DESAT blanking is shortened or replaced by fast current sensing; GaN devices have little or no short-circuit capability and rely on fast current sensing in the converter.

The gate loop on the board

The gate current flows from the driver, through the resistor, into the gate and back from the source to the driver. Any inductance in that loop slows switching and causes ringing that can push the gate past its limits. Worse is common-source inductance: inductance shared by the gate loop and the power path. The load current's di/dt induces a voltage in it that subtracts from the gate drive. Just 5 nH at 2 A/ns is 10 V, enough to slow switching badly or set off oscillations.

  • Use devices with a Kelvin source pin and return the driver to it, not to the power source connection.
  • Place the driver within a few millimetres of the switch, with gate and return traces stacked on adjacent layers to minimise loop area.
  • Decouple the driver supply with ceramic capacitors right at its pins; peak gate currents come from them, not from the supply.
Exercise 1: a faster turn-on

For the SiC example, what external gate resistor gives a 4 A peak gate current?

Answer

R_{g,ext} = 22/4 - 4 = 1.5\ \Omega, ignoring the driver's own resistance. Faster switching, but expect more overshoot and EMI.

Exercise 2: a smaller bootstrap capacitor

If a 100 nF bootstrap capacitor is used with the same 62 nC charge per cycle, how much does it droop?

Answer

62\,\text{nC}/100\,\text{nF} = 0.62 V each cycle, from about 15 V. Acceptable if the UVLO threshold is well below 14 V, but with no margin for longer on-times.

Exercise 3: is a Miller clamp needed?

An IGBT leg switches at 10 V/ns. The IGBT's C_{gc} is 50 pF, its internal gate resistance 2 Ω and the turn-off resistor 8 Ω, with a 0 V off-state. The threshold is 5.5 V. Is it at risk?

Answer

Injected current 50\,\text{pF} \times 10\,\text{V/ns} = 0.5 A, giving about 0.5 \times 10 = 5 V: dangerously close to 5.5 V, especially as the threshold falls with temperature. Add a Miller clamp or a negative turn-off voltage.

Key takeaways

  • The gate is a capacitor: peak current is \Delta V_{drive}/R_g, and drive power is Q_g \Delta V f_s.
  • The gate resistor trades switching loss against overshoot, ringing and EMI; use separate turn-on and turn-off paths.
  • Each device family has its own drive voltages; SiC wants +15–20 V / −2–5 V, GaN has almost no margin above 6 V.
  • Fast dv/dt can turn on an off switch through its Miller capacitance; use negative bias or a Miller clamp.
  • High-side switches need a floating supply: a bootstrap for cost, an isolated driver and supply for SiC and safety.
  • Drivers provide UVLO, desaturation detection and soft turn-off; layout with a Kelvin source and a tiny gate loop.
