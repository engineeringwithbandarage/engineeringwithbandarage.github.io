Thermal Design, Reliability & Protection

A PV inverter is expected to work for twenty years or more, outdoors, through hundreds of thousands of temperature swings, lightning surges, faults on the grid and faults in the array. Efficiency gets a converter sold; reliability and protection keep it running. This final chapter brings our 3 kW inverter's losses together, designs its cooling, estimates how long its weakest parts will last, and lists the protections that stop a fault becoming a fire.

The loss budget

Start every thermal design with a table of where the watts go at full power, on the hottest day:

The 30 W of semiconductor loss goes into one shared heatsink. The rest heats the enclosure's internal air, which is why the "ambient" for the semiconductors is not the outdoor temperature but the air inside the box, typically 10–20 K hotter.

One heatsink, several devices

Chapter 3 sized a heatsink for one device. With several, each device has its own junction-to-heatsink path, but they all share the heatsink-to-ambient resistance, so every device's loss heats every other device.

Junction temperature on a shared heatsink

T_{j,i} = T_a + R_{th(h\text{-}a)} \sum_k P_k + P_i \left( R_{th(j\text{-}c),i} + R_{th(c\text{-}h),i} \right)

Check the device with the highest P_i \times R_{th(j\text{-}h),i}: it runs hottest.

Worked example: the inverter's heatsink

Each device has R_{th(j\text{-}c)} = 0.5 K/W and a 0.3 K/W insulating pad, as in Chapter 3; the enclosure air reaches 50 °C.

  1. Hottest device: the boost MOSFET, 6 \times 0.8 = 4.8 K above the heatsink.
  2. Absolute limit: for T_j ≤ 125 °C the heatsink may reach 120.2 °C, so R_{th(h\text{-}a)} \le (120.2 - 50)/30 = 2.3 K/W.
  3. Reliability target: designing for T_j ≤ 100 °C at full sun instead gives R_{th(h\text{-}a)} \le (95.2 - 50)/30 = 1.5 K/W. That is a large finned heatsink with natural convection, or a much smaller one with a fan.

Why aim 25 K below the limit? Because lifetime, as the next sections show, depends steeply on temperature, and a fan is itself a wear part that many outdoor PV inverters prefer to avoid.

Thermal cycling and power-module lifetime

Steady temperature is only half the story. Every morning the inverter heats up and every evening it cools; every passing cloud makes the junction temperature swing in seconds. Silicon, copper, solder and aluminium bond wires expand at different rates, so each swing strains the joints between them. After enough cycles, bond wires lift off and solder layers crack: the main wear-out mechanism of power semiconductors.

How fast the junction follows a power change is described by the transient thermal impedance Z_{th(j\text{-}c)}(t) in the datasheet: within milliseconds only the die heats up; after seconds the case and heatsink follow. Short cloud transients therefore produce junction swings that the heatsink's large thermal mass does not smooth out.

Coffin–Manson cycling lifetime (simplified)

N_f = A \, \Delta T_j^{-n}

Cycles to failure fall steeply with the swing \Delta T_j; n is typically around 4–6 for bond wires and solder. Manufacturers publish power-cycling curves that also depend on the mean temperature and the cycle duration.

With n = 5, halving the swing from 60 K to 30 K multiplies the number of cycles to failure by 2^5 = 32. Over twenty years a PV inverter sees about 7,300 daily cycles, plus far more small cloud-driven ones. Lower losses, lower thermal resistance and devices with sintered silver die attach or copper bond wires all reduce wear.

Capacitor lifetime

The 1.2 mF DC link of Chapter 6 is usually a bank of aluminium electrolytic capacitors. Their electrolyte slowly evaporates through the seal, faster when hot; as it does, capacitance falls and ESR rises, which heats them further. They are, with fans, the classic life-limiting part.

Electrolytic lifetime (10-degree rule)

L = L_0 \cdot 2^{(T_0 - T_{hs})/10}

L_0: rated life at the rated temperature T_0; T_{hs}: the hot-spot temperature in use. Lifetime doubles for every 10 K cooler.

Hot-spot temperature from ripple current

T_{hs} = T_a + I_{rms}^2 \, ESR \, R_{th}

The 100 Hz ripple (5.3 A rms in our inverter) and the switching ripple both heat the capacitor from inside; share them across several parallel capacitors.

A capacitor rated for 5,000 h at 105 °C, running with a 65 °C hot spot, lasts 5000 \times 2^{4} = 80{,}000 h. A PV inverter operates about 4,400 hours a year (daylight), so that is about 18 years. Let the hot spot reach 75 °C and it falls to 40,000 h, about 9 years: keep the DC-link capacitors away from the heatsink and the inductors. Film capacitors avoid electrolyte wear-out and fail gracefully by self-healing, but store less energy per volume. They become practical when the DC link is smaller: in three-phase inverters (no 100 Hz pulsation), or when a small active power-decoupling circuit absorbs the single-phase ripple instead.

Voltage derating and cosmic rays

High-energy neutrons from cosmic rays occasionally trigger a destructive breakdown in a power device that is blocking a high voltage. The failure rate is negligible at moderate voltages but rises steeply as the DC voltage approaches the rating, and it is higher at altitude. Manufacturers publish failure-in-time (FIT) curves against DC voltage. That, plus switching overshoot (Chapter 10) and surges, is why designs keep the steady DC voltage well below the rating: 650 V devices on a 400 V link, and 1200 V devices on 800 V links.

Protection

Protections act on very different timescales, from nanoseconds in the gate driver to seconds in the grid monitoring, so they are implemented at different levels: hardware comparators for the fast ones, the controller's firmware for the rest, and a hardware watchdog that forces all switches off if the firmware stops responding.

PV strings cannot be switched off

As long as light falls on the panels, the string produces its open-circuit voltage: our string's 360 V (almost 400 V on a cold morning) is present on the DC cables even when the inverter is off or disconnected. Rapid-shutdown rules (NEC 690.12 in the US) and module-level shutdown devices exist for firefighter safety; in every other case, treat PV DC cabling as always live.

Exercise 1: adding a fan

A fan brings the heatsink's thermal resistance down to 0.8 K/W. What are the heatsink and boost MOSFET junction temperatures at full power?

Answer

T_h = 50 + 30 \times 0.8 = 74 °C, and T_j = 74 + 6 \times 0.8 = 78.8 °C: much better for cycling lifetime, as long as the fan lasts. Fan lifetime is itself rated in hours, so it needs a temperature sensor to detect a failed fan and derate.

Exercise 2: a hotter capacitor

Laid out next to the boost inductor, the DC-link capacitors run with a 75 °C hot spot. What is their expected life in years of PV operation?

Answer

5000 \times 2^{(105-75)/10} = 40{,}000 h, divided by 4,400 h a year: about 9 years, half of the 18 years at 65 °C. Moving them 10 K cooler doubles the inverter's life.

Exercise 3: insulation threshold

An inverter accepts PV strings up to 600 V. What minimum insulation resistance to earth must it measure before connecting?

Answer

600\,\text{V}/30\,\text{mA} = 20 kΩ. Below that, it reports an insulation fault and does not start.

Key takeaways

  • Start with a loss budget; devices on a shared heatsink heat each other through R_{th(h\text{-}a)}.
  • Design junctions well below their maximum: ours needs about 1.5 K/W for 100 °C at full sun.
  • Thermal cycling wears out bond wires and solder; cycles to failure fall roughly as \Delta T_j^{-5}.
  • Electrolytic capacitor life halves for every 10 K hotter; keep the DC link cool, or use film capacitors where the design allows.
  • Derate voltage to 60–70 % for semiconductors, for overshoot, surges and cosmic-ray robustness.
  • Protection is layered from nanoseconds to seconds: desaturation, overcurrent, overvoltage, surge, insulation, residual current, anti-islanding and arc detection.
