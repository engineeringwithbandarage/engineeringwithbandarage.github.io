Maximum Power Point Tracking

A PV string does not have a fixed output voltage. It has a curve, and on that curve there is exactly one point where it delivers the most power. That point moves every time a cloud passes or the panels warm up. Maximum power point tracking (MPPT) is the control algorithm that keeps the boost converter from Chapter 4 sitting on that point, and it is worth several percent of the energy a system produces in a year.

Running example:8 × 375 W panels in series+PV boost, 300 V → 400 V, 50 kHzThe same 3 kW string as Chapter 4. Its model and the MPPT simulation are in Python below.

The PV curve

A solar cell is a large diode that light turns into a current source. The simplest useful model, the single-diode model, says the string current is the light-generated current minus what leaks back through the diode:

Single-diode PV model

I = I_{ph} - I_0 \left( e^{V / (n N_s V_T)} - 1 \right), \qquad I_{ph} \approx I_{sc} \cdot \frac{G}{1000\ \text{W/m}^2}

N_s cells in series, diode ideality factor n, thermal voltage V_T = kT/q \approx 25.7 mV at 25 °C, irradiance G. Full models add a series and a shunt resistance.

Fitting this model to our string (open-circuit voltage V_{oc} = 360 V, short-circuit current I_{sc} = 10.75 A) gives a maximum power point (MPP) at 300 V and 10.0 A: 3.0 kW at standard test conditions. Multiply voltage by current along the curve and you get the power curve in Figure 5.1.

Three facts from the curve shape every MPPT design:

  • Irradiance moves the current, not the voltage. Photocurrent is proportional to sunlight, but V_{oc} only falls logarithmically. At half sun our MPP moves from 300 V to 286 V while the power falls to 1.42 kW.
  • Temperature moves the voltage. Crystalline silicon loses roughly 0.3 % of V_{oc} and about 0.4 % of its MPP voltage per kelvin. On a hot roof with cells at 65 °C, the MPP drops to about 300 \times (1 - 0.004 \times 40) \approx 252 V.
  • The MPP sits near 80 % of V_{oc}. To the left of it the string behaves like a current source; to the right, like a voltage source with power collapsing quickly.

Size the string for the coldest morning

Cold raises the voltage. At −10 °C our string's V_{oc} rises to about 360 \times (1 + 0.003 \times 35) \approx 398 V, and the converter sees that voltage at sunrise before it starts drawing current. The input capacitors, the boost switch and the diode must all survive the highest open-circuit voltage of the year, not the MPP voltage. This is why string length is checked against the site's record low temperature.

How the converter moves the operating point

The DC link is held at 400 V by the inverter (Chapter 6). The boost converter therefore controls its input voltage: in steady state V_{pv} = (1 - D)\,V_{dc}, so raising D pulls the PV voltage down and lowering D lets it rise. In practice a fast inner loop regulates the PV voltage to a reference (Chapter 11), and the MPPT algorithm, running far more slowly, decides what that reference should be.

Duty cycle for a given PV voltage

D = 1 - \frac{V_{pv}}{V_{dc}}

300 V on a 400 V link: D = 0.25. On the hot roof, 252 V: D = 0.37. At half sun, 286 V: D = 0.29.

Perturb and observe

The most widely used algorithm is also the simplest. Change the voltage reference by a small step, wait for the converter to settle, measure the power. If power went up, keep stepping the same way; if it went down, reverse. The operating point climbs the power curve and then oscillates around its top.

"""Perturb-and-observe MPPT on a model of the 3 kW PV string (8 x 375 W panels)."""
import math

A = 22.556          # n * Ns * kT/q for the whole string (V), fitted so the MPP is 300 V / 10 A
ISC, VOC = 10.75, 360.0
I0 = ISC / (math.exp(VOC / A) - 1)


def pv_current(v, sun=1.0):
    """Single-diode model; sun = irradiance relative to 1000 W/m2."""
    return max(sun * ISC - I0 * (math.exp(v / A) - 1), 0.0)


def perturb_and_observe(steps=200, step_v=2.0, cloud_at=100):
    v, p_old, direction, log = 250.0, 0.0, +1, []
    for k in range(steps):
        sun = 1.0 if k < cloud_at else 0.5
        p = v * pv_current(v, sun)            # measure after the converter has settled
        if p < p_old:
            direction = -direction             # power fell: reverse the perturbation
        p_old = p
        log.append((k, v, p, sun))
        v += direction * step_v                # new PV voltage reference for the boost converter
    return log


def best_power(sun):
    return max(v / 10 * pv_current(v / 10, sun) for v in range(1, int(VOC * 10)))


if __name__ == "__main__":
    log = perturb_and_observe()
    for sun, part in ((1.0, log[50:100]), (0.5, log[150:])):
        avg = sum(p for _, _, p, _ in part) / len(part)
        print(f"sun {sun:.0%}: tracked {avg:.0f} W of {best_power(sun):.0f} W available "
              f"({avg / best_power(sun):.2%}), PV voltage {min(r[1] for r in part):.0f}-{max(r[1] for r in part):.0f} V")
$ python pv_mppt.py
sun 100%: tracked 2999 W of 2999 W available (99.98%), PV voltage 298-302 V
sun 50%: tracked 1421 W of 1422 W available (99.98%), PV voltage 284-288 V

Choosing the step and the rate

  • Step size trades speed against oscillation loss. Oscillating ±2 V around 300 V costs our string about 0.5 W on average; ±5 V costs about 3.2 W, still only 0.1 %, but a large step tracks a moving MPP much faster. Many products use a variable step: large far from the peak, small near it.
  • Update rate must be slower than the converter's settling time, or the algorithm compares powers measured mid-transient. With a voltage loop settling in a few milliseconds, MPPT typically updates every 10–100 ms.
  • Moving irradiance is P&O's known weakness. If the sun brightens during a step, the power rises regardless of the step direction and the algorithm can walk away from the MPP. Taking an extra measurement without perturbing (so-called dP-P&O) separates the change caused by the sun from the change caused by the step.

Incremental conductance

At the MPP the slope of the power curve is zero. Since P = VI:

MPP condition

\frac{dP}{dV} = I + V \frac{dI}{dV} = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{dI}{dV} = -\frac{I}{V}

Left of the MPP, dI/dV > -I/V: increase the voltage. Right of it, decrease. At it, stop.

For our model at 300 V, the slope dI/dV is −0.0333 S and -I/V = -10/300 is −0.0333 S: the condition holds. The incremental conductance (IncCond) method compares these two numbers from successive measurements. Its advantage is that it knows when it has arrived and can stop perturbing, and it distinguishes a change in sunlight (current changes while voltage stays the same) from its own step. Its disadvantage is that a derivative amplifies measurement noise, so it needs good sensing and filtering.

Partial shading and the global peak

Each panel has bypass diodes across groups of cells. When two of our eight panels are shaded to 30 %, the string current can only exceed their reduced current if their bypass diodes take over, removing those panels from the string. The result is the dashed curve in Figure 5.1, with two peaks:

PeakVoltagePowerWhat is happening
Global224 V2.24 kWShaded panels bypassed; six panels at full current
Local324 V1.03 kWAll eight panels in series, limited to the shaded panels' current

A P&O or IncCond tracker that was running near 300 V before the shadow arrived climbs the nearest hill and settles happily on the 1.03 kW local peak, losing more than half the available power. Remedies:

  • Periodic global scan. Every few minutes, sweep the voltage across most of its range in a fraction of a second, note the highest power, then resume local tracking from there.
  • Several MPPT inputs. Strings on different roof faces get their own boost converter and tracker.
  • Module-level electronics. Power optimisers or microinverters give every panel its own MPPT, at higher cost per watt.

The sensing electronics

The algorithm is only as good as its measurements of PV voltage and current.

ADC resolution

\text{LSB} = \frac{V_{FS}}{2^{N}}

A 12-bit converter scaled to 500 V full scale resolves 0.12 V. A 2 V MPPT step is about 16 LSB: well above quantisation, but noise and ripple must be filtered.

Shunt sizing

V_{sh} = I R_{sh}, \qquad P_{sh} = I^2 R_{sh}

5 mΩ at 10 A gives 50 mV for the amplifier and dissipates 0.5 W. Hall-effect or fluxgate sensors avoid the loss and provide isolation.

  • Voltage: a high-value resistor divider (several resistors in series to share the voltage and survive surges) into a differential or isolated amplifier.
  • Current: a shunt with a current-sense amplifier when the measurement is referenced to the converter ground, or a Hall-effect sensor when isolation is needed.
  • Ripple: the boost inductor current has a 2 A peak-to-peak ripple at 50 kHz. Sample at the middle of the switch on-time, where the instantaneous current equals the average, or average over a whole number of switching periods.

Tracking performance is measured with the procedure of EN 50530, which defines static MPPT efficiency (steady sun) and dynamic efficiency (ramps of irradiance). Good commercial trackers exceed 99 % on both.

Exercise 1: duty cycle on a hot day

On a hot afternoon the MPP of our string is 252 V. The DC link is 400 V. What duty cycle does the boost run at, and what is its input current if the string delivers 2.5 kW?

Answer

D = 1 - 252/400 = 0.37. Input current 2500/252 \approx 9.9 A, almost the same as at 25 °C: heat costs voltage, not current.

Exercise 2: the cost of shading

With two panels shaded, a tracker without a global scan stays on the local peak. How much power does it lose, as a fraction of what is available?

Answer

2.24 - 1.03 = 1.21 kW, which is 54 % of the 2.24 kW available. A scan every few minutes recovers almost all of it.

Exercise 3: which way to step?

An IncCond tracker measures 280 V and 10.2 A. A small step gives dI/dV = -0.02 S. Should the voltage go up or down?

Answer

-I/V = -10.2/280 = -0.0364 S. The measured slope, −0.02 S, is greater than −0.0364 S, so the operating point is left of the MPP: increase the voltage.

Key takeaways

  • The PV string has one maximum power point, near 80 % of V_{oc}; sunlight moves its current, temperature moves its voltage.
  • The boost converter sets the PV voltage through its duty cycle, V_{pv} = (1-D)V_{dc}; MPPT chooses the reference.
  • Perturb and observe climbs the power curve with fixed steps and holds over 99.9 % of the available power in steady sun.
  • Incremental conductance uses dI/dV = -I/V to know when it has arrived.
  • Partial shading creates several peaks; a periodic global scan avoids getting stuck on a local one.
  • Sample the current at the centre of the on-time and size the string for the coldest morning's open-circuit voltage.
