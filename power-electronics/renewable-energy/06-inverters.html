Chapter 6 · Converter Circuits

Inverters

The boost converter delivers the string's power to a 400 V DC link. The grid wants a 50 Hz sine wave. An inverter bridges the two by switching the DC link across its output in a pattern whose average follows a sine wave, then filtering away the switching ripple. This chapter builds the single-phase and three-phase bridges, explains how their PWM patterns are generated, and sizes the DC-link capacitor of our 3 kW inverter.

The half-bridge and the H-bridge

One leg on its own, a half-bridge, connects its midpoint to either the positive or the negative DC rail. Measured from the midpoint of a split DC link, its output is \pm V_{dc}/2. Two legs make the H-bridge: the load sits between the two midpoints, and the output v_{AB} can be +V_{dc}, -V_{dc} or zero. The H-bridge uses the whole DC link, so it is the standard single-phase PV inverter.

Modulation index and output amplitude

m_a = \frac{\hat{V}_{ref}}{\hat{V}_{carrier}}, \qquad \hat{V}_1 = m_a V_{dc} \;\;(\text{H-bridge}), \qquad \hat{V}_1 = m_a \frac{V_{dc}}{2} \;\;(\text{half-bridge})

Linear for m_a \le 1. Above 1 (overmodulation) the output grows more slowly and low-order harmonics appear.

The 230 V grid peaks at \sqrt{2} \times 230 = 325 V, so on our 400 V link the inverter runs at m_a = 325/400 = 0.81, leaving margin for high grid voltage and the voltage drop across the filter.

Sinusoidal PWM

Sinusoidal PWM (SPWM) compares a sine reference with a high-frequency triangular carrier: the switch is on while the reference is above the carrier. The pulse widths then vary sinusoidally, and the average output over each switching period follows the reference.

  • Bipolar PWM switches the two legs as diagonal pairs (S1 with S4, S2 with S3). The output jumps between +V_{dc} and -V_{dc}, with ripple at the switching frequency f_s.
  • Unipolar PWM drives each leg from its own comparison, leg A with +m \sin\omega t and leg B with -m \sin\omega t. The output steps between +V_{dc} and 0 in the positive half-cycle, and between 0 and -V_{dc} in the negative one: three levels, with ripple at 2 f_s.

The ripple matters because it sets the size of the output inductor. With the output switching between two levels a step \Delta V apart, the peak-to-peak current ripple is greatest at 50 % duty:

Maximum ripple, bipolar PWM

\Delta I_{max} = \frac{V_{dc}}{2 L f_s}

Two-level output, ripple at f_s.

Maximum ripple, unipolar PWM

\Delta I_{max} = \frac{V_{dc}}{8 L f_s}

Half the voltage step at twice the frequency: a quarter of the ripple.

Our inverter delivers 3000/230 = 13.0 A rms, or 18.4 A peak. Allowing a ripple of 20 % of the peak (3.7 A) at f_s = 20 kHz needs L = 400 / (8 \times 3.7 \times 20\,\text{kHz}) \approx 0.68 mH with unipolar PWM, but 2.7 mH with bipolar PWM. That fourfold difference is why unipolar PWM is the default, with one important exception covered in Chapter 10: in a transformerless PV inverter, unipolar switching makes the panels' voltage to earth jump at the switching frequency, driving leakage current through the panels' capacitance. Topologies such as H5 and HERIC add switches to keep the unipolar ripple without that common-mode jump.

The three-phase bridge

Three legs, one per phase, make the three-phase inverter used above a few kilowatts. Each leg's midpoint swings between the rails, and the load sees the differences between legs. With SPWM, each phase reaches m_a V_{dc}/2, so the line-to-line voltage is limited to:

Three-phase output with SPWM

V_{LL,rms} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2\sqrt{2}}\, m_a V_{dc} \approx 0.612\, m_a V_{dc}

Space-vector PWM or third-harmonic injection raise the linear limit by 2/\sqrt{3}, i.e. 15.5 %, to V_{LL,rms} = 0.707\,V_{dc}.

For a 400 V (line-to-line rms) grid, whose line voltage peaks at 566 V, SPWM needs at least 653 V of DC link; space-vector PWM needs only 566 V. That 15 % is free performance, which is why three-phase inverters almost all use it, and why three-phase PV inverters run their DC link around 600–800 V.

Space-vector PWM

Each of the three legs is either up (1) or down (0), giving eight switching states. Six of them produce a voltage vector pointing in one of six directions; two (000 and 111) produce zero. Space-vector PWM treats the desired three-phase output as a single rotating vector and, in each switching period, builds it from the two neighbouring active vectors and a zero vector, each applied for the right fraction of the period.

Space-vector dwell times (sector 1, 0 \le \theta \le 60^\circ)

T_1 = T_s \frac{\sqrt{3}\,|V_{ref}|}{V_{dc}} \sin(60^\circ - \theta), \quad T_2 = T_s \frac{\sqrt{3}\,|V_{ref}|}{V_{dc}} \sin\theta, \quad T_0 = T_s - T_1 - T_2

Splitting T_0 equally between 000 and 111 gives the symmetric pattern with the lowest ripple.

Dead time: the distortion you build in

Chapter 3 showed that each leg needs a dead time t_d between turning one switch off and the other on, and that this shifts the average output by about t_d f_s V_{dc}. With 500 ns at 20 kHz on 400 V that is 4 V, and its sign follows the direction of the current, so it reverses at every current zero crossing. The result is a flattening of the current near zero and low-order harmonics (3rd, 5th, 7th). Controllers compensate by adding the expected error to the reference according to the current's sign, and the closed current loop (Chapter 11) removes most of what remains. Faster devices with shorter safe dead times reduce the problem at the source.

The DC link of a single-phase inverter

A three-phase inverter draws constant power from its DC link. A single-phase one cannot: its output power is p(t) = v \cdot i = P\,(1 - \cos 2\omega t), pulsating between zero and twice the average, at twice the grid frequency. The PV string supplies a steady P, so the DC-link capacitor must absorb the difference, storing energy in one half of each 10 ms period and returning it in the other.

DC-link capacitance for single-phase power pulsation

C_{dc} = \frac{P}{2\pi f_{grid} \, V_{dc} \, \Delta V_{pp}}

From the energy swing P/\omega = C V_{dc} \Delta V_{pp}. The capacitor also carries a 100 Hz ripple current of P/(\sqrt{2}\,V_{dc}) rms, plus the switching-frequency current.

For our inverter, allowing a 20 V peak-to-peak ripple on 400 V: C = 3000/(2\pi \times 50 \times 400 \times 20) \approx 1.2 mF, carrying 3000/(\sqrt{2} \times 400) \approx 5.3 A rms at 100 Hz. Compare that with the 9.4 µF the boost stage alone needed in Chapter 4: the inverter, not the boost, sizes the DC link. A millifarad at 450 V is almost always an electrolytic capacitor bank, which becomes a lifetime concern in Chapter 12.

Why the two-stage design helps MPPT

In a two-stage inverter the boost holds the PV voltage at its reference while the DC link swings with the 100 Hz ripple, so the panels never see it. A single-stage inverter, where the string connects straight to the DC link, passes the ripple onto the panels and loses a little energy oscillating around the MPP, unless the DC link is made larger.

Connecting to the grid

A grid-tied inverter does not set its output voltage: the grid does. It controls its output current, injecting a sine wave in phase with the grid voltage (unity power factor) or deliberately shifted when the grid operator asks for reactive power. A phase-locked loop (PLL) tracks the grid's phase and frequency; the current controller (Chapter 11) makes the inductor current follow the reference; and an outer loop adjusts the current's amplitude to hold the DC link at 400 V, so that all the power the boost delivers flows on to the grid.

Grid codes such as IEEE 1547 and IEC 61727 limit the current's total harmonic distortion (typically below 5 %), set the power factor and reactive-power behaviour, and require the inverter to stop within two seconds if the grid disappears (anti-islanding, Chapter 12).

Exercise 1: modulation index

The same 400 V-link inverter is installed on a 240 V grid. What modulation index does it need at nominal voltage, and how much margin is left before overmodulation?

Answer

Peak grid voltage \sqrt{2} \times 240 = 339 V, so m_a = 339/400 = 0.85. Only 15 % margin remains for grid overvoltage and the filter's voltage drop, which is why many designs raise the link to 420–450 V.

Exercise 2: halving the ripple

How much DC-link capacitance does our 3 kW inverter need for a 10 V peak-to-peak ripple?

Answer

C = 3000/(2\pi \times 50 \times 400 \times 10) \approx 2.4 mF: halving the ripple doubles the capacitance. The 100 Hz ripple current stays 5.3 A rms.

Exercise 3: a 480 V three-phase grid

What is the minimum DC-link voltage for a three-phase inverter on a 480 V (line-to-line rms) grid, with SPWM and with space-vector PWM?

Answer

Line peak 480\sqrt{2} = 679 V. SVPWM needs V_{dc} \ge 679 V; SPWM needs 2/\sqrt{3} times more, 784 V, before any margin.

Key takeaways

  • An H-bridge produces \hat{V}_1 = m_a V_{dc}; our 230 V inverter runs at m_a = 0.81 on 400 V.
  • Unipolar PWM gives three levels and ripple at 2f_s: a quarter of the bipolar ripple for the same inductor.
  • Space-vector PWM gets 15.5 % more output from a three-phase bridge than SPWM.
  • Dead time causes a current-dependent voltage error and low-order harmonics; compensate it.
  • Single-phase power pulsates at twice grid frequency; the DC-link capacitor (about 1.2 mF here) absorbs it.
  • A grid-tied inverter controls current, synchronised by a PLL, to the limits set by grid codes.
