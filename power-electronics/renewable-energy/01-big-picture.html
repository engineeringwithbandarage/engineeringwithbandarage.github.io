The Big Picture

Tools:Worked examples by hand+Python simulations in Chapter 11This chapter is conceptual: no equipment or software is needed to follow it.

A solar panel, a wind turbine and a battery each produce or store electricity in a form the grid can't use directly. The power converter between them and the grid does the translation: it changes voltage, current, frequency and even the direction of power flow, while wasting as little energy as possible. This chapter shows what those converters do, why they switch on and off thousands of times a second, and how the pieces fit into one system.

What power electronics does

Power electronics is the use of semiconductor switches to convert electrical energy from one form to another. "Form" means four things: whether it is DC or AC, its voltage, its frequency, and which way the power flows. Every converter does one of four basic conversions, and larger systems chain them together:

ConversionNameIn renewable systems
AC → DCRectifierThe generator side of a variable-speed wind turbine; a grid-powered battery charger

AC → ACUsually AC → DC → AC (back-to-back converter)Connecting a turbine's variable-frequency AC to the fixed-frequency grid

Why renewables can't skip the converter

Conventional power stations use large synchronous generators that turn at a speed locked to the grid frequency, so they connect almost directly. Renewable sources don't behave like that:

  • Solar panels produce DC, and the voltage at which they deliver the most power moves with sunlight and temperature all day long. Without a converter that keeps finding that point, the panel delivers noticeably less energy than it could.
  • Wind turbines capture the most energy when the rotor speed follows the wind speed. A variable speed means a variable output frequency, which must be converted to the grid's fixed 50 or 60 Hz.
  • Batteries are DC, their voltage changes as they charge and discharge, and power must flow in both directions under strict current and voltage limits.
  • The grid expects a clean sine wave at a fixed voltage and frequency, synchronised in phase, with limited harmonics. Grid codes also require the converter to stay connected through short faults and to disconnect safely when the grid is lost.

The converter is also the control system

Because the converter sits between the source and the grid, it decides how much power flows, when and in which direction. That is why a modern inverter does far more than convert: it tracks maximum power, supports grid voltage with reactive power, rides through faults and protects people working on the lines.

Why converters switch

The obvious way to reduce a voltage is to put a resistor or a transistor in series and drop the difference across it. That is a linear regulator, and its loss is simply the voltage it drops times the current through it:

Power and efficiency

P = V \cdot I, \qquad \eta = \frac{P_{out}}{P_{in}}

Efficiency η is the fraction of input power that reaches the output. Everything else becomes heat.

Linear regulator loss

P_{loss} = (V_{in} - V_{out}) \cdot I

The whole voltage difference is burned off as heat in the series device.

A switching converter does something cleverer. Its transistor is either fully on, so it has almost no voltage across it, or fully off, so almost no current flows through it. In both states the product of voltage and current, which is the power lost in the switch, is close to zero. Inductors and capacitors then smooth the chopped waveform into the voltage you want. The average output depends on the fraction of time the switch is on, the duty cycle D. For the simplest step-down converter, the buck, the relationship is:

Ideal buck converter (Chapter 4)

V_{out} = D \cdot V_{in}, \qquad 0 \le D \le 1

Switch on for half of each cycle (D = 0.5) and a 400 V input gives a 200 V output, ideally with no loss at all.

The linear design wastes as much power as it delivers and needs a heatsink to get rid of 2 kW. The switching design loses about fifty times less. For a renewable system that runs for decades, that difference is the whole business case. The remaining losses come from the switches not being perfect, which is exactly what Chapter 3 is about.

Efficiency multiplies along the chain

Energy from a PV panel usually passes through several conversion stages before it reaches the grid. The overall efficiency is the product of the stage efficiencies, so every stage matters:

Efficiency of a conversion chain

\eta_{total} = \eta_1 \cdot \eta_2 \cdot \ldots \cdot \eta_n

Example: boost at 98 %, inverter at 98 % and filter plus transformer at 99 % give 0.98 × 0.98 × 0.99 ≈ 95.1 % overall.

This is why system designers ask whether a stage can be removed. A PV inverter whose panels already deliver a high enough voltage can skip the boost stage, and a transformerless inverter saves the transformer's losses, at the cost of extra measures to handle leakage current and safety.

The same building blocks are arranged differently depending on the size of the installation:

What every converter must do

  1. Convert efficiently. Choose the right switches and keep conduction and switching losses low. Every percent of loss is energy not sold and heat that must be removed. Chapters 2–4
  2. Get the most from the source. Hold the panels at their maximum power point as conditions change. Chapter 5
  3. Produce clean waveforms. Turn DC into a low-distortion sine wave, or into the isolated voltage the next stage needs. Chapters 6–7
  4. Drive the switches safely. Turn each transistor on and off quickly, without shoot-through, and shut it down within microseconds of a fault. Chapter 8
  5. Keep it compact and quiet. Design the inductors and transformers, filter the ripple and lay out the board so it passes EMI tests. Chapters 9–10
  6. Stay in control and survive for decades. Close the control loops, and keep every component cool and protected for a twenty-year life outdoors. Chapters 11–12
Exercise 1: how much reaches the grid?

A 10 kW PV array feeds a boost converter with 98.5 % efficiency, followed by an inverter with 97.5 % efficiency. How much power reaches the grid, and how much is lost as heat?

Answer

Overall efficiency is 0.985 × 0.975 = 0.960375, so about 9.60 kW reaches the grid and about 396 W is lost as heat, split between the two converters.

Exercise 2: how high must the DC link be?

A single-phase full-bridge inverter connects to a 230 V (rms) grid. Its output can't exceed its DC-link voltage. What is the minimum DC-link voltage, and why do designs use more?

Answer

The grid's peak voltage is \sqrt{2} \times 230 \approx 325 V, so the DC link must stay above about 325 V at every instant. Designs add margin for grid voltage that runs above nominal, for the voltage drop across the filter, and for ripple on the DC link, which is why such inverters run their DC link noticeably higher.

Exercise 3: name the conversions

Which conversions are needed to (a) charge a home battery from the grid, (b) send PV power to the grid through a string inverter with a boost stage, and (c) connect a variable-speed wind turbine with a permanent-magnet generator?

Answer

(a) AC → DC, usually followed by DC → DC to control the charging current. (b) DC → DC (boost with MPPT), then DC → AC. (c) AC → DC on the generator side and DC → AC on the grid side: a back-to-back converter sharing one DC link.

Key takeaways

  • Power electronics converts between AC and DC, between voltages and frequencies, and in either direction, using semiconductor switches.
  • Solar, wind and batteries all need converters, because none of them naturally match the grid.
  • Converters switch fully on and off, which wastes far less power than dropping voltage across a resistor or transistor.
  • Efficiency multiplies along a conversion chain, so designers remove stages where they can.
  • The converter is also the control and protection system between the source and the grid.
