Chapter 10 · Hardware Design

Filters, EMI & PCB Layout

Switching is what makes converters efficient, and also what makes them noisy. Every edge produces current ripple that the grid must not see, high-frequency noise that must not leave the box, and voltage spikes that the switches must survive. This chapter designs the grid filter of our 3 kW inverter, explains how electromagnetic interference (EMI) escapes and how to stop it, and gives the layout rules that prevent most problems from appearing in the first place.

From L filter to LCL filter

Chapter 6 sized a single inductor for the inverter: 0.68 mH limits the ripple to 3.7 A peak-to-peak at 20 kHz with unipolar PWM. That is 20 % of the peak current: far too much ripple to inject into the grid, where it would heat cables, disturb other equipment and fail harmonic and EMC requirements. Making the inductor ten times larger would work but would be heavy, lossy, and would drop too much voltage at 50 Hz. The standard answer is the LCL filter: split the inductance in two and put a capacitor between them, so the ripple current takes the low-impedance path into the capacitor instead of the grid.

LCL resonant frequency

f_{res} = \frac{1}{2\pi} \sqrt{\frac{L_1 + L_2}{L_1 L_2 C_f}}

Place it between about ten times the grid frequency and half the ripple frequency, away from both.

Ripple reaching the grid

\frac{i_g}{i_1} = \frac{1}{\left| 1 - \omega_{sw}^2 L_2 C_f \right|}

Evaluated at the ripple frequency, 2 f_s for unipolar PWM.

Base values for sizing C_f

Z_b = \frac{V_{grid}^2}{P}, \qquad C_b = \frac{1}{\omega_{grid} Z_b}

Keep C_f below about 5 % of C_b, so the reactive current it draws at 50 Hz stays small.

Passive damping resistor

R_d \approx \frac{1}{3\,\omega_{res} C_f}

In series with C_f. Active damping in the controller can replace it and save its loss.

Worked example: the 3 kW LCL filter

  1. Inverter-side inductor: keep L_1 = 680 µH from Chapter 6, which limits the bridge's ripple to 3.7 A peak-to-peak.
  2. Capacitor limit: Z_b = 230^2/3000 = 17.6\ \Omega, C_b = 1/(2\pi \times 50 \times 17.6) = 180 µF. Choosing C_f = 4.7 µF (2.6 % of C_b) draws only 0.34 A and 78 var at 50 Hz.
  3. Grid-side inductor: choose L_2 = L_1/4 = 170 µH.
  4. Resonance: f_{res} = \tfrac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{850\,\mu\text{H}/(680\,\mu\text{H} \times 170\,\mu\text{H} \times 4.7\,\mu\text{F})} = 6.3 kHz: above 500 Hz and below 10 kHz (half the 20 kHz switching frequency). ✓
  5. Attenuation: at the 40 kHz ripple, \omega^2 L_2 C_f = 50.5, so only 1/49.5 = 2.0 % of the ripple reaches the grid: 0.074 A peak-to-peak instead of 3.7 A.
  6. Damping: R_d = 1/(3 \times 2\pi \times 6.3\,\text{kHz} \times 4.7\,\mu\text{F}) = 1.8\ \Omega. The ripple current through it costs up to about 2 W; that loss is why many designs use active damping instead.

Why the resonance needs damping

At 6.3 kHz an undamped LCL filter has almost zero impedance from the bridge's point of view and a large peak in its transfer function. Any controller or grid harmonic near that frequency is amplified, and the current loop of Chapter 11 can become unstable. Damping it, passively or actively, is not optional.

Grid codes (IEEE 1547, IEC 61000-3-2 and -3-12, and national rules) limit the current's individual harmonics and its total harmonic distortion, typically to 5 % or less at rated power, with tighter limits on higher-order harmonics. The filter handles the switching-frequency ripple; the current controller and dead-time compensation handle the low-order harmonics.

Leakage current in transformerless PV inverters

A PV array is a large metal-backed surface close to an earthed frame or roof. Between the cells and earth there is a distributed capacitance, typically 50–150 nF per kilowatt of panels, much more when wet. If the inverter makes the array's average voltage to earth (its common-mode voltage) jump, current flows through that capacitance to earth.

Capacitive leakage current

i_{leak} = C_{pv} \, \frac{dv_{cm}}{dt}, \qquad v_{cm} = \frac{v_{AN} + v_{BN}}{2}

With bipolar PWM, v_{cm} stays at V_{dc}/2; with plain unipolar PWM it jumps by V_{dc}/2 at the switching frequency.

For our 3 kW array with 300 nF to earth, a common-mode square wave of ±200 V at 20 kHz (fundamental amplitude \tfrac{4}{\pi} \times 200 = 255 V) drives roughly 2\pi \times 20\,\text{kHz} \times 300\,\text{nF} \times 255\,\text{V} \approx 9.6 A. Safety standards (IEC 62109-2, VDE 0126-1-1) require the inverter to disconnect if residual current exceeds 300 mA, or jumps by 30 mA. This is why transformerless single-phase inverters use bipolar PWM or the H5, HERIC and similar topologies, which disconnect the bridge from the grid during the zero states so the common-mode voltage stays constant while keeping the three-level output of unipolar PWM.

Conducted EMI

Electromagnetic compatibility standards (CISPR 11 for most inverters) limit the high-frequency noise a product conducts back into its supply and grid connections, measured between 150 kHz and 30 MHz through a line impedance stabilisation network (LISN). Noise travels two ways:

Differential mode (DM)
Current flowing out on one line and back on the other: the switching ripple and its harmonics. Attenuated by X capacitors (line to line) and inductance in each line.
Common mode (CM)
Current flowing out on all lines together and returning through earth, driven by dv/dt at the switch node through parasitic capacitances: device tab to heatsink (typically 50–100 pF through an insulating pad), transformer interwinding capacitance, the PV array's capacitance. 100 pF at 50 V/ns is a 5 A spike. Attenuated by a common-mode choke and Y capacitors (line to earth).

Second-order filter corner and attenuation

f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}, \qquad \text{attenuation} \approx 40\ \text{dB/decade above } f_c

A common-mode choke of 5 mH with two 4.7 nF Y capacitors gives f_c \approx 23 kHz and about 32 dB at 150 kHz.

Y capacitors are limited by the touch current they allow to flow to earth at 50 Hz, so most of the common-mode attenuation must come from the choke, usually on a nanocrystalline or high-permeability ferrite core. The cheapest EMI reduction, though, is at the source: smaller switch-node copper area, slower edges where efficiency allows, and earthed shields under the switch tabs that return CM current locally.

PCB layout rules

At SiC and GaN speeds, the layout is part of the circuit. Three loops matter most.

1. The power commutation loop

When a switch turns off, its current transfers to the complementary diode or switch through a loop that includes the DC-link decoupling capacitor. Any inductance in that loop stores energy that appears as a voltage overshoot and ringing on the switch.

Turn-off overshoot

\Delta V = L_{loop} \, \frac{di}{dt}

20 nH at 2 A/ns adds 40 V on top of the DC link.

Ringing frequency

f_{ring} = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{L_{loop} C_{oss}}}

20 nH with 150 pF rings at about 92 MHz, right in the radiated-emission band.

  • Place small ceramic or film decoupling capacitors directly across each half-bridge, millimetres from the switches; the bulk electrolytics are too far away and too inductive to supply the edge current.
  • Route the forward and return paths on adjacent layers, one above the other, so their fields cancel. A well-designed SiC power loop is under 5 nH.
  • An RC snubber across the switch, sized to the ringing frequency, damps what remains.

2. The gate loop

As Chapter 8 explained: the driver next to the switch, a Kelvin source connection, gate and return traces stacked, and no shared path with the power current.

3. The switch node

The switch node is the source of almost all common-mode noise: it swings hundreds of volts in nanoseconds. Keep its copper area as small as current and cooling allow, keep sensitive signals away from it, and do not route it over or near control circuitry.

Grounding and sensing

  • Separate the power return from the control ground and join them at one deliberate point, usually at the current-sense resistor or the controller's power-ground pin.
  • Take voltage and current measurements with Kelvin connections and differential traces, filtered at the ADC with a small RC.
  • Place heatsinks and shields so they are earthed or tied to a quiet node, never left floating near the switch node.
Exercise 1: a smaller grid inductor

If L_2 is reduced to 85 µH with the same L_1 and C_f, what are the new resonant frequency and ripple attenuation at 40 kHz?

Answer

f_{res} = \tfrac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{765\,\mu\text{H}/(680\,\mu\text{H} \times 85\,\mu\text{H} \times 4.7\,\mu\text{F})} \approx 8.5 kHz, still below 10 kHz. \omega^2 L_2 C_f = 25.2, so 1/24.2 = 4.1 % of the ripple reaches the grid, about 0.15 A peak-to-peak: twice as much ripple, half the grid-side inductor.

Exercise 2: heatsink capacitance

Six TO-247 switches each have 80 pF from tab to an earthed heatsink. The switch nodes swing at 30 V/ns. What peak common-mode current flows, assuming two nodes switch together?

Answer

Only the switches whose tab is the switch node matter, here two at a time: 2 \times 80\,\text{pF} \times 30\,\text{V/ns} = 4.8 A in nanosecond spikes. An earthed shield layer in the insulating stack, returned to the DC link, keeps this current local.

Exercise 3: halving the loop

A redesign cuts the power-loop inductance from 20 nH to 5 nH. What happens to the overshoot at 2 A/ns and to the ringing frequency with 150 pF?

Answer

Overshoot falls from 40 V to 10 V. The ringing frequency doubles, to about 184 MHz, with a quarter of the energy (\tfrac{1}{2}LI^2), and is easier to damp.

Key takeaways

  • An LCL filter diverts switching ripple into C_f: ours passes only 2 % of the 40 kHz ripple to the grid.
  • Place the LCL resonance between 10× grid frequency and half the ripple frequency, and damp it.
  • Common-mode voltage on a PV array drives leakage current through its capacitance; transformerless inverters keep it constant.
  • Conducted EMI travels in differential and common mode; X capacitors and inductors handle DM, a CM choke and Y capacitors handle CM.
  • Minimise the power loop, the gate loop and the switch-node area: \Delta V = L_{loop}\,di/dt.
