Magnetics: Inductors & Transformers

Inductors and transformers are usually the largest, heaviest and most expensive parts of a converter, and unlike switches and capacitors they are often designed for the job rather than picked from a catalogue. This chapter gives you the physics in three equations, the materials to choose from, where the losses come from, and then designs the 750 µH inductor of our PV boost converter from scratch.

Three equations

Faraday: voltage sets flux change

v = N A_e \frac{dB}{dt}

The voltage across N turns on a core of effective area A_e. Volts × seconds determine the flux swing.

Flux density from current

B = \frac{L \, i}{N A_e}

Follows from N\Phi = Li. The peak current sets the peak flux density, which must stay below saturation.

Inductance of a gapped core

L = \frac{\mu_0 N^2 A_e}{l_g + l_e/\mu_r} \approx \frac{\mu_0 N^2 A_e}{l_g}

l_g: total air-gap length; l_e: magnetic path length; \mu_r: relative permeability of the core (thousands for ferrite).

Why inductors have air gaps

A ferrite core without a gap would give enormous inductance from a few turns, but it would saturate at a tiny current. Saturation is the core running out of magnetic domains to align: above the saturation flux density B_{sat}, the permeability collapses, the inductance falls towards that of an air coil, and the current rises almost unchecked. In a converter that means an overcurrent within microseconds.

An air gap trades inductance for current capacity. The energy stored, \tfrac{1}{2}LI^2, is held almost entirely in the gap's volume, so the gap length determines how much energy the core can store before saturating. Transformers, which ideally store no energy, are normally ungapped. The flyback, which is really a coupled inductor, is gapped.

Ferrite's saturation falls as it heats, so designs use a peak of about 0.3 T at operating temperature, leaving margin for current transients.

Where the losses come from

Core loss

Each flux cycle costs energy in hysteresis and eddy currents. Manufacturers characterise it with the empirical Steinmetz equation:

Steinmetz core-loss equation

P_v = k \, f^{\alpha} \, \hat{B}^{\beta}

Loss per volume; \hat{B} is the peak AC flux amplitude. For ferrites, \alpha is typically 1.1–1.8 and \beta 2.3–3, from the datasheet's loss curves.

The key word is AC. A DC-carrying inductor with small ripple, like our boost inductor, has a large DC flux but a small flux swing, and core loss depends on the swing. A transformer, whose flux swings fully in both directions, is limited by core loss long before saturation, which is why transformers run at 0.05–0.15 T at 100 kHz.

Winding loss

DC resistance

R_{dc} = \rho \frac{N \cdot MLT}{A_{cu}}, \qquad \rho_{Cu} = 1.72 \times 10^{-8}\ \Omega\text{m at 20 °C}

MLT: mean length of one turn. Copper's resistance rises by 0.39 % per kelvin: 31 % more at 100 °C.

Skin depth in copper

\delta \approx \frac{66}{\sqrt{f}} \ \text{mm} \quad (f \text{ in Hz})

0.30 mm at 50 kHz, 0.21 mm at 100 kHz. AC current crowds into this layer, so conductors much thicker than 2\delta waste copper at high frequency.

Two effects raise AC resistance: the skin effect within each conductor, and the proximity effect, where the fields of neighbouring layers push current to one side of each conductor, worsening rapidly with the number of layers. Remedies are Litz wire (many insulated strands thinner than \delta, twisted so each strand takes every position), copper foil for high currents, and interleaving primary and secondary layers in transformers.

The area-product method

The core must have enough cross-section to carry the flux and enough window to hold the copper. Multiplying the two requirements gives a single number for choosing a core size:

Area product for an inductor

A_p = W_a A_e \ge \frac{L \, I_{pk} \, I_{rms}}{B_{max} \, J \, K_u}

W_a: window area; J: current density (3–5 A/mm²); K_u: window fill factor (0.3–0.5 for round wire on a bobbin).

Design example: the PV boost inductor

Chapter 4 specified 750 µH carrying 10 A with 2 A peak-to-peak ripple at 50 kHz, peaking at 11 A. The RMS current is \sqrt{10^2 + 2^2/12} = 10.02 A.

  1. Core size: with B_{max} = 0.3 T, J = 4 A/mm² and K_u = 0.4: A_p \ge 750\,\mu\text{H} \times 11 \times 10.02 / (0.3 \times 4\,\text{A/mm}^2 \times 0.4) = 17.2 cm⁴. An E65/32/27 ferrite pair, with A_e = 535 mm² and roughly 21 cm⁴ of area product on its bobbin, fits. (Always confirm against the core's own datasheet.)
  2. Turns: N = L I_{pk}/(B_{max} A_e) = 750\,\mu\text{H} \times 11/(0.3 \times 535\,\text{mm}^2) = 51.4, so 52 turns, giving a peak of 0.297 T.
  3. Gap: l_g = \mu_0 N^2 A_e / L = 4\pi \times 10^{-7} \times 52^2 \times 535\,\text{mm}^2 / 750\,\mu\text{H} = 2.4 mm in the centre leg. Fringing flux around the gap raises the inductance a little, so in practice the gap is adjusted after measuring the first sample.
  4. Copper: 10 A at 4 A/mm² needs 2.5 mm² per turn, 130 mm² in total, which needs 325 mm² of window at a fill of 0.4: within the bobbin's winding area. The current is almost pure DC (its ripple is only 0.58 A rms), so skin effect barely matters here: several parallel strands of ordinary magnet wire, each under about 0.6 mm (2\delta), are enough.
  5. Winding loss: with a mean turn of about 150 mm, 52 turns is 7.8 m; R_{dc} = 1.72 \times 10^{-8} \times 7.8/2.5\,\text{mm}^2 = 54 mΩ at 20 °C and 71 mΩ at 100 °C, so 10.02^2 \times 0.071 \approx 7.1 W.
  6. Core loss: the flux swing is \Delta B = L \Delta I/(N A_e) = 750\,\mu\text{H} \times 2/(52 \times 535\,\text{mm}^2) = 54 mT peak-to-peak, an amplitude of only 27 mT. On a power ferrite at 50 kHz that is a fraction of a watt.

Total loss is about 7 W, or 0.24 % of 3 kW, almost all of it in the copper. That is typical of a well-designed DC inductor: once the core is chosen, the trade-off is copper weight against copper loss. A powder core (sendust, for example) is a common alternative: its higher saturation allows fewer turns and less copper, at the price of inductance that falls by 20–40 % at full current, which must be included in the ripple calculation.

Transformers

A transformer's primary turns are set by Faraday's law and the flux swing the core can tolerate, which at high frequency is limited by core loss, not saturation. For a full bridge applying a square wave of \pm V:

Primary turns for a square-wave drive

N_p = \frac{V}{4 f_s \hat{B} A_e}

From Faraday's law over a half-period: the flux swings from -\hat{B} to +\hat{B} in 1/(2f_s).

For the 3 kW LLC transformer of Chapter 7 (400 V, 100 kHz, ratio 8.33) on the same size of core: at 0.1 T, N_p = 400/(4 \times 100\,\text{kHz} \times 0.1 \times 535\,\text{mm}^2) = 18.7. The turns ratio must also be an integer ratio, so choose 25 primary and 3 secondary turns (8.33), which lowers the flux to 75 mT and the core loss with it. The 3-turn secondary carries about 69 A rms (a near-sinusoidal current is \pi/(2\sqrt{2}) times the 62.5 A DC output) and is wound with copper foil or Litz; interleaving primary and secondary sections reduces the proximity loss, while a deliberate separation between them can provide the LLC's resonant inductance L_r as leakage, saving a component.

Exercise 1: pushing the flux harder

Redesign the boost inductor's turns and gap for a peak flux of 0.35 T on the same core. What is the risk?

Answer

N = 750\,\mu\text{H} \times 11/(0.35 \times 535\,\text{mm}^2) = 44.06, so 45 turns (0.343 T), with a gap of 4\pi \times 10^{-7} \times 45^2 \times 535\,\text{mm}^2/750\,\mu\text{H} = 1.8 mm. Less copper and loss, but at 100 °C ferrite saturates around 0.38 T, leaving little margin for current transients.

Exercise 2: Litz strands for 100 kHz

What is the largest sensible strand diameter for Litz wire in the LLC transformer at 100 kHz?

Answer

\delta = 66/\sqrt{100\,000} = 0.21 mm, so strands no thicker than about 0.2 mm; 0.1 mm is common, to handle the proximity effect in many-layer bundles too.

Exercise 3: a missing gap spacer

The inductor is assembled without its gap. What happens when the boost starts?

Answer

The inductance becomes very large but the core saturates at a tiny fraction of the current. As soon as the current rises, the inductance collapses, the current shoots up within one switching period, and only the overcurrent protection (Chapter 12) saves the switch.

Key takeaways

  • B = Li/(NA_e) sets the turns for a given peak flux; the air gap l_g \approx \mu_0 N^2 A_e/L sets the inductance and stores the energy.
  • Design ferrite inductors for about 0.3 T peak at temperature; saturation means sudden overcurrent.
  • Core loss depends on the AC flux swing; winding loss on DC resistance plus skin and proximity effects.
  • The area product A_p \ge L I_{pk} I_{rms}/(B_{max} J K_u) picks the core size.
  • Our boost inductor: E65 ferrite, 52 turns, 2.4 mm gap, about 7 W, nearly all in the copper.
  • Transformers are limited by core loss; N_p = V/(4 f_s \hat{B} A_e), with interleaving and Litz or foil to control AC loss.
