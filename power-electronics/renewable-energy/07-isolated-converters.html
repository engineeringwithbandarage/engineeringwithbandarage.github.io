Isolated DC-DC Converters

The converters of Chapter 4 connect input and output through a common wire. Many renewable systems cannot allow that: a 48 V battery that people touch must be separated from a 400 V DC link, a panel-level converter must multiply 40 V by ten, and every inverter needs small, separated supplies for its control and gate drivers. Putting a transformer inside the converter solves all three. This chapter builds the main isolated topologies and designs a resonant converter that charges a battery from our DC link.

Why isolate

  • Safety. Galvanic isolation keeps a dangerous voltage off circuits people can touch, such as a low-voltage battery bank or communication ports.
  • Large voltage ratios. A transformer's turns ratio does the heavy lifting, so the switches run at a sensible duty cycle. Chapter 4 showed that a boost cannot efficiently reach a gain of ten; a 1:10 transformer does it easily.
  • Grounding freedom. The output can be referenced to any potential: earthed, floating, or stacked on another supply.
  • Several outputs from one converter, each with its own winding: the classic auxiliary supply.

The transformer inside a converter

An ideal transformer scales voltage by its turns ratio. A real one adds two parasitic inductances that dominate converter design:

Magnetising inductance L_m
The current needed to magnetise the core. Volt-second balance applies to it exactly as to any inductor: the average voltage across the winding must be zero, or the flux ratchets up each cycle until the core saturates. Every isolated topology needs a way to reset the core.
Leakage inductance L_{lk}
Flux that links one winding but not the other. When a switch interrupts the current, the energy in the leakage, \tfrac{1}{2} L_{lk} I^2, produces a voltage spike that must be clamped, or, in resonant and soft-switching converters, put to work.

The flyback

The flyback is a buck-boost whose inductor has a second winding. While the switch is on, current builds in the primary and energy is stored in the core's air gap; the secondary diode is reverse-biased. When the switch turns off, the energy "flies back" through the secondary into the output. The transformer is really a coupled inductor, and its core resets naturally during the off-time.

Flyback conversion ratio (CCM)

V_{out} = V_{in} \, \frac{N_s}{N_p} \, \frac{D}{1 - D}

The buck-boost ratio scaled by the turns ratio.

Flyback switch stress

V_{DS} = V_{in} + \frac{N_p}{N_s} V_{out} + V_{spike}

The reflected output adds to the input. The leakage spike is limited by an RCD clamp or an active clamp.

Worked example: the auxiliary supply

The inverter's control board and gate drivers need 15 V, about 10 W, taken from the 400 V DC link.

  1. Turns ratio: aiming for D ≈ 0.35, N_p/N_s = 400 \times 0.35 / (15 \times 0.65) = 14.4; choose 14. The actual duty cycle is 210/(400 + 210) = 0.34.
  2. Reflected voltage: 14 \times 15 = 210 V, so the switch sees 610 V before the leakage spike.
  3. Switch: with a clamp limiting the spike to about 100 V, the switch peaks near 710 V. An 800 V MOSFET is the cost-driven minimum; a 1 kV or 1.2 kV SiC device gives the voltage margin recommended in Chapter 12. Additional secondary windings provide further isolated outputs, such as the high-side gate-driver supplies of Chapter 8.

Simple and cheap, the flyback dominates below about 150 W. Above that, its single-ended energy storage makes the core and the output capacitor too large.

The forward converter

The forward converter is an isolated buck: the transformer transfers energy directly while the switch is on, and an output inductor smooths it. Because the transformer does not store the energy, its core must be reset separately, usually by a third winding that returns the magnetising energy to the input.

Forward conversion ratio

V_{out} = D \, V_{in} \, \frac{N_s}{N_p}, \qquad D \le \frac{1}{2} \;\; (\text{1:1 reset winding})

The reset needs as long as the on-time, so the duty cycle is limited to 0.5, and the switch blocks 2V_{in}.

Two-switch and active-clamp variants lower the switch stress and extend the duty cycle. Forward converters cover roughly 100–500 W.

The phase-shifted full bridge

For kilowatts, a full bridge drives the transformer with a symmetrical square wave, which uses the core in both directions and resets it automatically. The two legs run at a fixed 50 % duty cycle, and the output is controlled by shifting the phase of one leg relative to the other, which sets the effective duty cycle D_{eff}.

Phase-shifted full bridge

V_{out} = \frac{N_s}{N_p} \, V_{in} \, D_{eff}

During the phase-shift transitions, the leakage inductance's energy discharges the switches' output capacitances, giving zero-voltage switching (ZVS) at medium and high load.

The LLC resonant converter

The LLC converter places a resonant tank, a capacitor C_r and an inductor L_r (often the transformer's own leakage), in series with the transformer, whose magnetising inductance L_m forms the second "L". The bridge produces a 50 % square wave; the output is controlled by changing the frequency.

Using first-harmonic approximation (treating the square wave as its fundamental), the gain depends on three normalised quantities:

LLC gain (first-harmonic approximation)

M = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\left(1 + \lambda - \dfrac{\lambda}{f_n^2}\right)^2 + Q^2\left(f_n - \dfrac{1}{f_n}\right)^2}}

f_n = f_s/f_r with f_r = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{L_r C_r}); \lambda = L_r/L_m; Q = \sqrt{L_r/C_r}/R_{ac} with R_{ac} = 8 n^2 R_{load}/\pi^2. M = 1 corresponds to V_{out} = V_{in}/n for a full bridge.

Design example: 400 V to a 48 V battery

A 3 kW charger takes power from the 400 V DC link to a 48 V battery (42–56 V over its state of charge), with resonance at 100 kHz.

  1. Turns ratio for M = 1 at the nominal 48 V: n = 400/48 = 8.33.
  2. Load as seen by the tank: R = 48^2/3000 = 0.768\ \Omega, so R_{ac} = 8 \times 8.33^2 \times 0.768/\pi^2 = 43.2\ \Omega.
  3. Tank: choosing Q = 0.4 at full load gives Z_r = \sqrt{L_r/C_r} = 0.4 \times 43.2 = 17.3\ \Omega, so L_r = Z_r/(2\pi f_r) = 27.5 µH and C_r = 1/(2\pi f_r Z_r) = 92 nF.
  4. Magnetising inductance: with L_m/L_r = 5, L_m = 138 µH.
  5. Check the range: the battery needs M from 42/48 = 0.875 (run above resonance) to 56/48 = 1.17 (below resonance). At full load the peak gain is 1.39 (Figure 7.2), so 1.17 is reachable with margin.
  6. Check ZVS: the magnetising current that discharges the bridge's capacitances in the dead time peaks at about n V_{out}/(4 L_m f_r) = 400/(4 \times 138\,\mu\text{H} \times 100\,\text{kHz}) \approx 7.3 A. That comfortably achieves ZVS, but it circulates without delivering power, so a larger L_m would cut conduction loss at the price of less gain range.

Stay to the right of the peak

Left of each curve's peak the tank looks capacitive: the current leads the voltage, ZVS is lost and the switches can suffer hard reverse recovery. The controller must limit the minimum frequency so the converter always operates on the right-hand (inductive) slope.

The dual active bridge

A battery must be charged and discharged. The dual active bridge (DAB) puts an active full bridge on both sides of the transformer, with an inductance L (leakage plus an external inductor) between them. Both bridges produce square waves; shifting one relative to the other by a phase angle \varphi drives power from the leading bridge to the lagging one.

DAB power transfer (single phase shift)

P = \frac{V_1 V_2' \, \varphi \, (\pi - |\varphi|)}{2\pi^2 f_s L}, \qquad P_{max} = \frac{V_1 V_2'}{8 f_s L} \;\text{at}\; \varphi = \pi/2

V_2' = n V_2 is the secondary voltage referred to the primary. The sign of \varphi sets the direction of power flow.

For 400 V to a 48 V battery (V_2' = 400 V) at 100 kHz, delivering 3 kW at \varphi = 30° needs L = 37 µH, giving a maximum of 5.4 kW at 90°. DABs achieve ZVS when the two voltages are matched (V_1 \approx V_2') and lose it at light load or mismatched voltage, which more advanced modulation schemes address.

Exercise 1: a flyback on an 800 V link

The same 15 V flyback with N_p/N_s = 14 is moved to a three-phase inverter with an 800 V DC link. What duty cycle does it run at, and what does the switch see before the spike?

Answer

D = 210/(800 + 210) = 0.21, and the switch sees 800 + 210 = 1010 V. A 1.7 kV SiC MOSFET, common in auxiliary supplies for 800 V systems, is needed.

Exercise 2: charging an empty battery

The LLC charger above starts charging a nearly empty battery at 42 V. Is it running above or below resonance?

Answer

Required gain 42/48 = 0.875 < 1, so above resonance, where all the curves in Figure 7.2 fall below 1. ZVS is kept; the rectifier loses its zero-current turn-off, so its diodes or synchronous rectifiers switch harder.

Exercise 3: the DAB at half power

What phase shift does the 37 µH DAB need to transfer 1.5 kW?

Answer

Solve \varphi(\pi - \varphi) = 1500 \times 2\pi^2 \times 100\,\text{kHz} \times 37\,\mu\text{H} / 400^2 = 0.685, giving \varphi \approx 0.236 rad, or 13.5°.

Key takeaways

  • Isolate for safety, large voltage ratios, grounding freedom and multiple outputs.
  • Every isolated converter must reset its core; leakage inductance causes spikes unless it is clamped or used for soft switching.
  • Flyback: V_{out} = V_{in}(N_s/N_p)\,D/(1-D), the standard auxiliary supply below about 150 W.
  • Forward: an isolated buck, limited to D ≤ 0.5 with a 1:1 reset winding.
  • LLC converters control output by frequency, are most efficient at resonance, and must stay on the inductive side of the gain peak.
  • The dual active bridge transfers power in either direction by phase shift: the natural battery interface.
