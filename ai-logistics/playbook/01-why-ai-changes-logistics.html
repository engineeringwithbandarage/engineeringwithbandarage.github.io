Why AI Changes Logistics

A logistics company sells a promise: this shipment, at this place, by this time, at this price. Keeping that promise means making millions of small decisions every day. For a century those decisions were made by planners, dispatchers, brokers and supervisors. AI moves them into software, and that changes far more than the cost of each decision.

~15 min read Book: chapters 1, 2 and 4

Two models every logistics company runs

Iansiti and Lakhani separate a firm into two parts, and the separation is the key to the whole book:

Business model: what you promise
How the company creates value for customers (next-day delivery, reliable lead times, end-to-end visibility) and how it captures some of that value (per-shipment rates, contract logistics fees, subscriptions, a broker's margin).
Operating model: how you deliver it
The systems, processes, assets and people that actually move the goods: the network, the fleet, the warehouses, the planning teams, the IT. The business model points to potential value; the operating model is what realises it and what limits it.

The authors' argument is that AI's biggest effect is on the operating model. In logistics that is obvious once you look: a "same-day delivery" promise is worthless without an operating model that can plan, route and pick fast enough to keep it.

The three operating challenges: scale, scope, learning

Every operating model is judged on three things. In logistics they look like this:

ChallengeWhat it meansIn logistics
ScaleDeliver more volume at lower unit costMore parcels, loads, TEUs or pallets through the same network; lower cost per stop, per mile, per pick
ScopeOffer a wider variety of servicesMore lanes, modes, customer segments and value-added services (returns, cold chain, customs, final mile, financing)
LearningImprove and innovate over timeBetter forecasts, tighter routes, fewer damages and delays, faster response to disruption

In a traditional company all three run into the same wall: complexity. More volume needs more dispatchers and planners; more lanes and services need more specialists and more coordination; learning is limited by how fast experienced people can spot patterns and pass them on. Past a point, each extra unit of growth makes the operation harder to manage. The book calls this diminishing returns to scale, scope and learning.

Taking people off the critical path

The book's central observation is that digital firms remove the human bottleneck from the critical path of delivering value. People still design the systems, handle exceptions and do physical work that machines cannot yet do, but the routine decision on each shipment is made by software. In the authors' words, employees "design and oversee a software-automated, algorithm-driven digital organization that actually delivers the goods."

Logistics examples from the book make the point:

  • Amazon. "From demand forecasting to warehouse management, and from supply chain management to capacity planning, software and AI are increasingly running the show." People work at the edge of the network on tasks computers cannot yet handle, such as picking an oddly shaped item, and often the computer tells them what to do, for example the optimal pick path.
  • Ocado. Described as "an AI company disguised as a supply chain company disguised as an online grocer." Routing algorithms run thousands of calculations per second across a fleet of thousands of vans; demand prediction tells refrigerated trucks when to collect from farms; algorithms coordinate thousands of warehouse bots. The book reports 98.5% on-time delivery. Its CTO's philosophy: "look for the next thing to automate … You never get to the end."
  • Uber. No dispatcher decides which car takes which ride. Matching, pricing and supply-demand prediction are all algorithmic.

Outside the book, UPS's ORION route-optimisation system is the classic logistics case: UPS reported it saves drivers roughly 5–8 miles a day, around 100 million miles and 10 million gallons of fuel a year once fully deployed, and that one mile per driver per day is worth up to $50 million a year. The point is not the algorithm itself; it is that one decision (the order of stops) is now made by software for every driver, every day, and improves as data accumulates.

Why logistics is different from pure software

Ant Financial can serve 700 million users with fewer than 10,000 staff because its product is information. Logistics always has a physical layer: trucks, drivers, docks, containers. AI cannot make that layer infinitely scalable. What it can do is take the decisions about that layer (planning, matching, routing, pricing, scheduling, exception handling) off the human critical path, and squeeze far more value from every physical asset. The physical layer becomes the bottleneck you manage; the decision layer stops being one.

Collisions are already happening

The book describes a collision: a firm with a digital operating model enters a market served by traditional firms. Value delivered by the digital model starts lower but rises faster, and once it passes critical mass the traditional firm struggles to respond, as Nokia did against iOS and Android. In logistics the collisions are visible:

  • Digital freight platforms and AI-heavy brokers against phone-and-email brokerages (chapter 7).
  • Retailers and platforms that built their own networks (Amazon Logistics is the obvious one) against traditional parcel carriers.
  • Automated fulfilment platforms licensed to retailers (Ocado's partnerships with Kroger, Sobeys and others) against conventional distribution centres.
  • Visibility and orchestration platforms that sit above carriers and capture the customer relationship, the "operating system" layer the book warns about in travel and smartphones.

The book is blunt about the response. Spinning off a digital unit, opening an innovation lab or buying a startup is not enough. The incumbent has to rebuild its operating model on a data and AI foundation. Chapters 2 and 3 describe that foundation; chapter 9 the journey.

What this means for a logistics leader

  1. AI is not a project; it is how the operation will run. Think "which decisions will software make, and how do our people supervise and improve them?" rather than "which AI tool should we buy?".
  2. Learning is the new advantage. Every shipment produces data. A company whose systems learn from every route, scan and delay gets better at a rate a people-only organisation cannot match.
  3. The prize compounds. One use case saves money. A shared data platform that feeds fifty use cases changes the shape of the value curve.

Key takeaways

  • The business model is the promise; the operating model delivers it. AI's deepest impact is on the operating model.
  • Traditional operations hit diminishing returns to scale, scope and learning because complexity grows with people. Digital operating models do not.
  • The goal is to move routine decisions off the human critical path while people design, supervise and handle exceptions.
  • Logistics keeps a physical bottleneck, but the decision layer above it (planning, routing, pricing, matching) can be digitised and made to learn.
