Warehouse and Fulfilment

The warehouse is where the book's "humans at the edge, software at the core" idea is most visible. Amazon and Ocado run fulfilment centres where algorithms decide where each item lives, which robot or person fetches it and in what order, while people handle the tasks machines still cannot. Most warehouses are far from that, which is exactly why the opportunity is large.

Slotting: where things live

Fast movers near dispatch, items ordered together stored together, heavy items low. Traditional slotting is reviewed occasionally; AI slotting re-evaluates continuously as demand shifts. The book's association-mining example is from a logistics-heavy retailer: Ocado learned from its data that diapers and beer were strongly related (new parents do not go out much). The same kind of affinity, found in your order history, decides which SKUs to slot side by side to shorten pick paths.

Pick paths, batching and labour

Walking is most of a picker's time. Optimising which orders to batch and the sequence to visit locations cuts travel; labour planning turns the volume forecast (chapter 4) into shifts, and real-time balancing moves people between zones as queues build. The book's Amazon description is telling: "many times, computers are defining what humans should do, not the other way around, as in figuring out the optimal path to find and pick a specific product."

Robotics and orchestration

Robots are physical, but what makes a robotic warehouse scale is software: deciding which robot does which task and how hundreds of them move without traffic jams.

  • Amazon announced its one millionth robot in mid-2025, alongside DeepFleet, a generative AI foundation model trained on its fulfilment data to coordinate robot movement. Amazon said it expects DeepFleet to improve robot fleet travel efficiency by about 10%, and reports that robots assist in roughly three quarters of its customer orders.
  • Ocado's fulfilment centres, as the book describes, can be the size of eleven football pitches, with thousands of bots coordinated by algorithms that prioritise urgent orders and avoid congestion, and around ten thousand grocery boxes moving at once.
  • DHL Supply Chain works with several robotics firms (Locus Robotics, Boston Dynamics, Robust.AI among them) and reports moving staff into robot supervision and system monitoring roles. Its "plug and play" orchestration platform, built with Microsoft and Blue Yonder, reportedly cut robot integration time by 60% in its first site in Madrid.
  • Maersk has deployed AI-enabled robotics in its logistics centres, including Berkshire Grey picking systems in the UK and Dexory inventory-scanning robots in Romania.

Book lens: the next thing to automate

Ocado's CTO describes automation as a journey without an end: "We start with the obvious thing and move on to automate the next thing and the next thing." Ocado's COO adds: "Human beings can do everything that AI can do. They just can't do it to scale." That is the right posture for a warehouse programme. Do not try to design the fully automated building on day one. Build the data and orchestration layer, automate one process, learn, and move to the next. Each step moves another task off the human critical path.

Computer vision

Cameras on fixed points, forklifts, drones and scanning robots count inventory, read labels, check pallet build and spot damage. Inventory accuracy is the quiet enabler: every planning and fulfilment algorithm assumes the system knows what is where. Vision-based cycle counting, as in Maersk's Dexory deployment, attacks this directly.

Receiving, dock and yard

Predicted ETAs from chapter 4 feed dock scheduling: assign doors and labour to the trucks that will actually arrive, not the appointments on paper. Yard management uses the same data to stage trailers and reduce driver waiting time and detention charges.

Warehousing as a platform

The book highlights that Ocado's capabilities enabled a second business model: licensing its technology platform to retailers such as Kroger in the US and Sobeys in Canada. For a 3PL this is a strategic question worth asking: is your warehouse AI only a cost saver, or a capability you can offer customers, for example inventory and service predictions they cannot produce themselves?

Key takeaways

  • Slotting, batching and labour planning are software-only wins available before any robot arrives.
  • Robotic scale is decided by orchestration software and data, not by the robots alone.
  • Automate one process at a time on a shared data and orchestration layer; people move to supervision and exceptions.
  • Inventory accuracy, increasingly via computer vision, underpins every other warehouse algorithm.
