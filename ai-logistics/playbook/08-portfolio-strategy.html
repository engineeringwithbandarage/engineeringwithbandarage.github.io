Chapter 08 · Transformation

Choosing the Use-Case Portfolio

Chapters 4–7 listed nearly thirty use cases. No company should start them all. This chapter gives a scoring method to choose and sequence them, then applies the book's strategic network analysis, which explains why some AI investments build a lasting advantage and others only keep you level with competitors.

~20 min read Book: chapters 5 and 6

Step 1: build a long list from the business, not the technology

When Fidelity launched its AI Center of Excellence, its leader started with small-group sessions by business unit and function to list key decisions, use cases and goals. "There was no shortage of applications," he recalled. Do the same: in each function, ask which repeated decisions drive cost, service or revenue, who makes them today, and what data exists about them. A use case is a decision, not a tool.

Step 2: score each use case

Score each candidate from 1 to 5 on four dimensions:

DimensionQuestions
ValueHow much cost, revenue, service or emissions impact, at full scale? How often is the decision made? Can you measure the effect?
FeasibilityDoes the data exist, is it clean and joined? How many systems must integrate? Is there a proven technique? Will users adopt it?
Strategic effectDoes it build learning or network effects (step 4)? Does it create reusable data and components for other use cases?
Risk (inverted)Safety, legal, customer or workforce impact if it goes wrong? Is a human easily kept in the loop?

Step 3: sequence for compounding, not just ROI

The most common mistake is funding each use case as an isolated project with its own data pipeline. Instead, prefer early use cases that create shared assets. Volume forecasting lands clean order and calendar data; ETA lands the shipment event stream; email automation lands customer and location master data. Each later use case reuses them, so cost per use case falls while value rises: the book's AI factory economics.

Step 4: strategic network analysis

The book argues that strategy in the age of AI shifts from analysing industries to analysing networks: the connections between a firm and its customers, suppliers and partners, and the data flowing through them. Two forces create value as networks grow:

Network effects
The service becomes more valuable as more participants join. A freight marketplace has indirect network effects: more shippers attract more carriers and vice versa. A shared pallet pool or a cross-carrier visibility platform can have similar effects.
Learning effects
The service improves as more data flows through it. More shipments mean better ETAs, prices and routes, which attract more shipments. Learning effects are strongest when the data is unique and the problem complex, and weakest for simple problems anyone can solve with public data.

What weakens the advantage

The book's analysis of Uber is directly relevant to logistics, because freight networks share many of its properties:

FactorBook exampleLogistics translation
ClusteringUber's network is local: drivers in Boston don't help riders in Detroit, so local rivals can competeLast-mile, drayage and regional trucking are clustered by city or port; scale in one region does not protect another. Global forwarding and air networks are less clustered.
MultihomingRiders and drivers use several ride apps, which squeezes marginsShippers use several brokers and carriers use several load boards; whoever offers the better price today wins. Lock-in comes from service, integration and data, not exclusivity.
DisintermediationHomejoy failed once customers and cleaners dealt directlyAfter a good match, shipper and carrier may bypass the broker. The book's answer (from ZBJ in China): add complementary services that keep value flowing through you, such as visibility, payments, factoring, insurance, claims, compliance.
Network bridgingAlibaba bridged e-commerce data into lending through Ant FinancialShipment and payment data can support carrier financing, fuel and maintenance programmes, insurance pricing, emissions reporting or demand insights sold to customers.

The strategic questions

The book closes its strategy chapter with a set of questions. Adapted for a logistics business:

  1. What is the core service delivered, and which decisions in it are you digitising?
  2. Which networks are key to it (shippers, carriers, drivers, consignees, ports)? Do they have strong learning or network effects? Are they clustered?
  3. If effects are weak, how can you strengthen them, for example by adding data no competitor has, or by connecting participants to each other?
  4. If value only appears at critical mass, how do you get there (anchor customers, your own fleet as the first supply)?
  5. Which secondary networks (insurers, financiers, equipment makers, regulators) could use your data or services?
  6. Where are you exposed to multihoming and disintermediation, and what complementary services counter them?
  7. Where are the best value capture opportunities, and which side has the least competition and the greatest willingness to pay?
  8. What network bridges could your data support?

Book lens: two ways to respond to a collision

Facing iOS and Android, Nokia had two options: build its own digital operating model, or become the best possible complement to the platforms, as Samsung did. It did neither and collapsed. Logistics incumbents face the same choice against digital platforms: build the platform yourself, or become the best-run, best-integrated asset operator that platforms depend on. Both can work. Drifting between them does not.

Key takeaways

  • Start from repeated business decisions; score on value, feasibility, strategic effect and risk.
  • Sequence so early use cases build shared data products that make later ones cheap.
  • Learning effects from unique operational data are the most defensible AI advantage in logistics.
  • Clustering, multihoming and disintermediation limit platform profits; complementary services and network bridging counter them.
