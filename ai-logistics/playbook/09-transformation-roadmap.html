Chapter 09 · Transformation

The Transformation Roadmap

Knowing the use cases is the easy part. Becoming a company that runs on them is a multi-year change in data, technology, organisation and culture. This chapter lays out the stages, the principles that separate successful transformations from expensive pilots, and a concrete first year.

~25 min read Book: chapters 5 and 10

Why it is worth it

The authors and Keystone Strategy assessed more than 350 enterprises on roughly forty business processes and built an AI maturity index. The top quartile clearly outperformed the bottom quartile:

Three-year averageLaggards (bottom 25%)Leaders (top 25%)
Gross margin37%55%
Earnings before taxes11%16%
Net income7%11%

Correlation is not proof that AI caused the gap, as the authors would be first to say. But leaders shared clear traits: one integrated data platform, a "single version of the truth", AI used both to automate decisions and to inform complex ones, and sensor and telemetry data used to optimise operations, all directly relevant to logistics.

The four stages

Most logistics companies today sit between stages 1 and 2: plenty of pilots, vendor tools and dashboards, but each with its own data. The book is clear that pilots meet little resistance because they change nothing structural. Stage 3 is the real test: aggregating data from all the silos, establishing one source of truth for volumes, costs, service and assets, usually with a central data and analytics team working hub-and-spoke with business units. Stage 4 adds a standard way of building, deploying, monitoring and governing AI, so dozens of applications can run on the same foundation.

Five principles for transformation

From Microsoft's transformation under Satya Nadella and their wider research, the authors draw five principles:

  1. One strategy. Clear, committed, long-term goals from the top, and unity: "not about spinning off an autonomous group, carving out an AI division, or setting up a skunkworks." Microsoft's Azure leader put it simply: "There is no plan B."
  2. Architectural clarity. Everyone must understand the target operating architecture: integrated data, a catalogue of where data lives, common standards. The authors' surprise: the IT organisation and CIO often resist, because traditional IT was built to run a back office, not to drive transformation.
  3. Agile, product-focused organisation. Small teams owning AI applications as products, led by people who understand the operation. Microsoft renamed IT "Core Services Engineering"; its leader said "our product is the process."
  4. Capability foundations. Hire and grow software, data and AI talent with proper career paths, and especially the data and AI product manager: someone with business background who identifies use cases and leads teams to deliver them.
  5. Clear, multidisciplinary governance. Legal, risk, operations and technology together set policies for privacy, safety, bias and security (chapter 10).

The GE Digital warning

The book's leadership chapter describes how GE Digital became a separate profit centre and was seen by other GE businesses as a competitor; they neither adopted its technology nor supported it. Billions were spent without cohesion. A logistics "digital unit" that sells to the operating divisions instead of changing them risks the same fate. Put the platform at the centre and make operations own the outcomes.

A first-year plan

PeriodFocusOutputs
Days 0–30Commit and diagnoseExecutive sponsor and AI charter; maturity assessment by process; data inventory and catalogue of systems; long list of use cases by function
Days 30–90Foundations and first winsScored portfolio (chapter 8); target architecture; master identifiers agreed; data platform landing zone; two or three wave 1 use cases in shadow mode; governance board and policies
Months 3–6Prove and scaleWave 1 in production with measured value; first shared data products (orders, shipment events, assets); AI literacy programme for managers; hiring of product managers and engineers
Months 6–12Build the factoryWave 2 use cases (ETA, pricing, routing) on the shared platform; experimentation practice (pilots, switchbacks, simulation); model monitoring; first AI agents on bounded tasks with human approval

Fidelity's path is a good template: a centre of excellence governed by senior leadership, an integrated customer view built first, its own analytics stack, agile teams in two-week sprints, every application tested on an experimentation platform before deployment, and hundreds of business leaders taught the basics of algorithms.

Measuring progress

  • Business outcomes: cost per shipment, stop or pick; on-time-in-full; empty miles; asset utilisation; breakdowns; quote win rate and margin; touches per shipment; emissions per tonne-km.
  • Capability: share of key decisions made or recommended by software; number of use cases in production on the shared platform; time from idea to production; data quality scores.
  • Adoption and trust: override rates by planners and drivers, and why; user satisfaction; incidents and model rollbacks.

Book lens: aligning the fleet

Vodafone's former CEO Vittorio Colao described transformation as a fleet of boats: some catch the new winds faster than others. Let each go at its own speed and the slow ones fall behind; force them into one programme and you risk a "linear speed" that disruptors outrun. In a logistics group with many business units, the answer is usually one shared platform and governance, with business units free to move at their own pace on top of it.

Key takeaways

  • Leaders in AI maturity showed much higher margins; the gap comes from integrated data and AI across many processes.
  • Pilots are easy; the data hub stage is where commitment is tested.
  • Follow the five principles: one strategy, architectural clarity, agile product teams, capability foundations, multidisciplinary governance.
  • Do not isolate digital in a separate unit; put the platform at the centre and make operations own the results.
