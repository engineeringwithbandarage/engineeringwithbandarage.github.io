Planning, Forecasting and Visibility

Planning sets the stage for everything else: how many trucks, drivers, dock doors and pickers you need, where inventory should sit, and what the network looks like. Visibility tells you, while the plan runs, where it is going wrong. Both are prediction problems, which makes them a natural first home for the AI factory.

Volume and demand forecasting

Every downstream decision depends on how much volume is coming. Traditional forecasts are spreadsheets built on last year plus a growth factor, adjusted by experienced planners. Machine learning forecasts combine many more signals (customer order patterns, promotions, holidays, weather, economic indicators) at a much finer grain: by lane, by depot, by day, even by hour.

The value comes downstream: fewer empty miles and underused shifts when the forecast is too high, fewer missed pickups and expensive spot capacity when it is too low. Secondary sources citing McKinsey put the impact of AI-enabled distribution at 5–20% lower logistics cost and 20–30% lower inventory; treat these as indicative ranges for building your own case.

Forecast distributions, not single numbers

For capacity and inventory decisions, the useful output is a range ("90% chance volume is between 1,800 and 2,300 stops"), not a single figure. Probabilistic forecasts let the planning optimiser trade off the cost of being short against the cost of being over-staffed.

Capacity and labour planning

Turn forecasts into resources: fleet size, driver rosters respecting hours-of-service rules, warehouse shifts, temporary labour, dock appointments. This is where forecasting plus optimisation earns its money, especially in peak seasons. Trade press summed up DHL's peak-season approach, built on predictive planning and robotics, as "more planning, less panic."

Network design and digital twins

Strategic decisions (opening a hub, closing a cross-dock, changing a linehaul schedule) used to be studied once every few years by consultants. A digital twin (a simulation model of the network kept up to date with live data) lets planners test scenarios continuously: a new customer, a port closure, an electric fleet with charging constraints, a carbon target. It is also the safest experimentation platform for decisions you cannot A/B test.

Predictive ETA

Customers rarely ask for faster; they ask for reliable and predictable. A carrier's planned ETA is a promise; a predicted ETA is an estimate learned from what actually happened on similar shipments. Models use live position (GPS for road, AIS for ships), traffic, weather, historical dwell at each facility, terminal and port congestion, and carrier performance.

  • In ocean freight, berth waiting at busy hubs such as Singapore can run to several days in peak periods; research at NTU uses machine learning on real arrival data at the Port of Singapore to predict actual arrival and turnaround times.
  • Forwarders now productise this: DHL offers a "Smart ETA" for port-to-port ocean shipments.
  • Better ETAs feed other use cases: dock scheduling, labour planning at the receiving warehouse, customer notifications and inventory decisions.

Define "arrival" before you predict it

ETA accuracy depends on the definition of the event. The Port of Rotterdam, for example, defines a vessel's arrival at the pilot boarding place. Is "delivered" when the truck arrives at the gate, docks, or is unloaded? Agree definitions across systems first, or the model will learn the noise in your data.

Disruption sensing, control towers and agents

A control tower brings shipment status, predictions and alerts into one view. The first generation simply displayed data and left people to spot problems. The AI version predicts which shipments will fail their promise, ranks them by business impact, and proposes (or takes) the corrective action: re-book on another sailing, expedite, re-sequence a route, warn the customer.

This is where AI agents are arriving fastest. Gartner's 2026 forecast expects supply chain leaders to prioritise "clusters of simple AI agents" to orchestrate multi-step workflows within 12–18 months, and expects spend on supply chain software with agentic AI to grow to $53 billion by 2030. LLMs also read the unstructured signals (news of a strike, a carrier's email about a cancelled sailing) that structured feeds miss.

Book lens: learning effects

Planning use cases have strong learning effects: more shipments mean more data, better forecasts and ETAs, and better service, which attracts more shipments. The book notes that prediction accuracy improves with data but levels off for simple problems; the defensible advantage comes from combining many models and from data competitors do not have, such as your own scan events and carrier performance history. That is why the data pipeline from chapter 2 is the real asset.

Key takeaways

  • Forecasting volume well is the foundation for capacity, labour, inventory and pricing decisions.
  • Use probabilistic forecasts and let optimisers trade off the cost of being short against being over.
  • Predictive ETA turns scattered tracking data into reliability customers value, and feeds docks, labour and notifications.
  • Control towers are evolving from dashboards to prediction plus action, the first natural home for logistics AI agents.
