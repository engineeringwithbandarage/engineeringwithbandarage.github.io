Transportation and Fleet

Moving vehicles is where most logistics cost sits: drivers, fuel, maintenance and the assets themselves. Small improvements per stop or per mile multiply across thousands of vehicles and millions of stops. This is the area with the longest track record of AI and optimisation in logistics.

Route optimisation: the reference case

UPS's ORION (On-Road Integrated Optimization and Navigation) is the best-documented example in the industry. It plans the stop sequence for each of tens of thousands of drivers every day. UPS reported savings of roughly 6–8 miles per driver per day, about 100 million miles and 10 million gallons of fuel a year at full US deployment, and noted that one mile per driver per day is worth up to $50 million a year. Later dynamic versions re-plan during the day as conditions change.

Lessons that apply to any fleet:

  • The optimiser is only as good as its inputs. Real service time at each stop, real travel time by time of day, real parking behaviour: these are learned from telematics and scan data, which is why route optimisation improves with the AI factory behind it.
  • Drivers must trust it. UPS spent years on adoption: explaining the routes, capturing driver knowledge as constraints, and allowing justified overrides. An optimal plan that drivers ignore saves nothing.
  • Optimise the right objective. Cost per stop, on-time rate, emissions, driver fatigue and customer time windows pull in different directions. Choosing the weights is a management decision, the "designer" role from chapter 3.

Dynamic dispatch

Static plans break: a truck is late, an order is added, a driver calls in sick. Dynamic dispatch re-assigns work continuously. The book's ride-hailing examples show the pattern: "dispatchers do not decide which car is chosen" on Uber, Grab or DiDi. In freight the same logic powers on-demand trucking, same-day courier networks and recovery of failed deliveries. Reinforcement learning helps where today's choice affects tomorrow's options, for example positioning empty vehicles where demand is likely to appear.

Load building and empty miles

Trailers that leave partly empty and trucks that drive back empty are pure waste. Optimisation packs loads respecting weight, stackability and drop sequence; matching engines find backhauls. Freight marketplaces add network effects here: the more carriers and shippers on a platform, the better the chance of a good match (chapter 8 discusses the strategic catch: these networks are geographically clustered).

Predictive maintenance

Calendar-based maintenance services vehicles too early or too late. Predictive maintenance watches engine fault codes and sensor data and schedules work before a failure strands a truck and its cargo. Reported results vary widely: an analytics provider cited about 20% fewer roadside breakdowns across the fleets it monitors, and a documented case for a 1,200-vehicle fleet reported 35% fewer breakdowns and about $2 million a year saved. Vendor claims above 50% are common but rarely independently verified.

Start with fault codes you already have

Most modern trucks already send diagnostic trouble codes through telematics. A first version can simply rank active codes by severity and history of leading to breakdowns, then route them to the workshop planner. That alone captures much of the value before you build a full failure-prediction model.

Fuel, electrification and emissions

AI helps on three fronts: driver behaviour (idling, speeding, harsh acceleration), route and speed choices, and, for electric fleets, planning around range, charger availability and electricity tariffs. Because fuel is one of the biggest cost lines, the same route-optimisation savings are also emissions savings, which many customers now require you to report.

Driver safety and coaching

Computer vision on dashcams detects distraction, phone use, tailgating and fatigue; telematics scores harsh events. Used well, it prevents accidents and gives coaching targeted to each driver. Used badly, it becomes surveillance that drivers resent and that can carry bias. Chapter 10 covers the governance: transparency to drivers, what is recorded, who sees it, and how scores are used in employment decisions.

Autonomous vehicles

The book points to autonomous driving as an extreme case of learning effects: varied, complex algorithms requiring huge amounts of real-time mapping and traffic data, which builds high barriers to entry. For most logistics companies the practical question is not building autonomy but preparing to use it: in yards, warehouses and ports first (already common), then on selected highway lanes. The preparation is the same AI factory: clean lane data, digital dispatch and APIs that an autonomous carrier can plug into.

Key takeaways

  • Route optimisation is proven at scale; its value depends on learned inputs and on driver adoption.
  • Dynamic dispatch removes the dispatcher from the routine decision and keeps plans valid as reality changes.
  • Predictive maintenance can start from existing fault codes; expect roughly 20–35% fewer breakdowns in documented cases.
  • Safety AI needs clear rules on driver data from day one.
